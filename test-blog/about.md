# About This Test Blog

# Sentence

Everything here was written for the test: ten plain articles of science, a set of diagrams, and three pages about the blog itself. None of it describes a real person, place or study.

# Paragraph

Everything here was written for the test. The ten articles explain ordinary science in plain words — light, ice, magnets, plants, storms and the Moon — and the pictures beside them were drawn as simple diagrams. The writing is synthetic: it was made to exercise the site, and it says nothing about any real person, place or study. Read the articles as a reader would. # Short

Everything in this blog was made for the test. Ten articles explain ordinary science in plain words: why the sky is blue, why ice floats on water, how magnets work, how plants make food, why thunder follows lightning, why the Moon changes shape, why sunsets are red, how a rainbow forms, why hot air balloons rise, and why we have seasons.

The writing is synthetic. It was written to exercise the site — the depth slider, the index, the static build — and it says nothing about any real person, place or study. The pictures were drawn for the same reason, each one showing a single idea as a plain diagram.

Three pages sit above the articles, in the row at the top of the index. They are pages rather than posts, so they carry no date, no tag and no reading time, and they do not join the list of articles. One of them explains [how to read the blog](/post/how-to-read); another is the [colophon](/post/colophon), which says how the site is built and published.

# Medium

This blog exists so the site can be tested on something real. The ten articles are the test: each one is written at all five depths the site knows, each one carries two to four pictures of its own, and each one links to the others. Together they are enough to tell whether the index, the slider, the build and the publishing all behave.

## What the articles are

The articles are ordinary science, explained without any jargon: light and colour, water and ice, magnetism, plant food, storms, balloons, seasons and the phases of the Moon. They are written to be read, not skimmed, and each one can be read at any of the five depths. The pictures are drawn as simple diagrams, one idea to a picture, and every one of them was made for the test.

## What the pages are

The three pages in the row at the top are pages, not posts. A pinned page drops the furniture of a post: no date, no tag, no reading time on the index, and no meta line on its own page. It keeps the slider, so it can still be read at five depths like everything else. This page explains the blog, [how to read the blog](/post/how-to-read) explains the depth slider, and the [colophon](/post/colophon) explains the machinery.

## What it is not

None of this is a real publication. The writing says nothing about any real person, place or study, and the diagrams are drawn to show an idea rather than to record anything. The blog is published only so that the site can be looked at in a browser, on a phone and in a plain static build.

# Long

This blog is a test bench. It was made to exercise a small static site generator, and everything in it was written for that purpose. Nothing here was commissioned, nothing here is a claim about the world, and nothing here should be quoted as authority on any subject.

## The articles

There are ten of them, and each one is written twice over at five depths: a single sentence, a single paragraph, three paragraphs, a page, and three pages. That is the whole point of the exercise. A generator can be written in an afternoon and will look right on one screen; the only way to know whether it holds up is to feed it enough writing to fill every stop on the slider and to see that the stops stay in order.

The subjects were picked for spread rather than for excitement. Light and colour take three articles on their own, because the same scattering explains the blue sky, the red sunset and the rainbow, and writing them separately is the only way to check that a link between two articles holds. Water and ice take two, for the same reason: the density of water is what makes ice float and what makes a hot air balloon rise. Magnetism, plant food, storms, the seasons and the phases of the Moon fill the rest.

Every article carries between two and four pictures of its own, drawn as simple diagrams that carry one idea each. The pictures are served from the same folder as the writing and are optimised on the way out, so the test also covers the media path and the size of a published page.

## The pages

Three pages stand above the articles in a row at the top of the index. They are pinned, which is the word the site uses for a page that is not a post. A pinned page keeps its place at the top whatever its date, drops the meta line a post carries, and joins the row rather than the list.

They exist because the row needed something to hold, and because a site that publishes only articles never exercises the row at all. This page says what the blog is; the [page on reading the blog](/post/how-to-read) explains the slider, the keyboard and the addresses; the [colophon](/post/colophon) explains the program, the build and the publishing.

## The rules the writing follows

The writing obeys the same rules as any other post, and the audit in the repository enforces them: a sentence of at most thirty-five words, a paragraph of fewer than a hundred and twenty, every depth present, every picture present in the pictures folder, and no word written twice in a row. The audit reads this folder as well as the real one, and it is the judge of whether the writing holds.

## What it is for

None of it is for a reader who wants to learn about the sky. It is for whoever is building the site: a set of files with enough text, enough links and enough pictures to make a broken page, a slow build or a bad index obvious. When the site breaks, this is what breaks with it.
