# How a Rainbow Forms

# Sentence

A rainbow is sunlight scattered by millions of water droplets, each a prism and a mirror at once, so the colours sit about 42 degrees around the point opposite the sun.

# Paragraph

A rainbow is sunlight scattered by millions of water droplets. Each droplet acts as a prism and a mirror at once. Light slows as it enters the water and bends, splitting into colours, then reflects off the back of the drop and bends again on the way out. Red is bent the least and violet the most, so red sits on the outside of the bow and violet on the inside. The ray turns about 138 degrees in all, which puts the bow about 42 degrees away from the point directly opposite the sun. The sun has to be behind you for any of it to show.

# Short

A rainbow is sunlight scattered by millions of water droplets. Each droplet does two jobs at once. It is a small prism, which bends and splits the light, and a small mirror, which reflects it back. Together they make a bow of colour in the sky.

The prism part comes first. Light moves faster through air than through water, so it slows down and turns as it passes into a drop. The turn is not the same for every colour. The index of water is about 1.343 for violet light and about 1.331 for red, so violet is bent the most and red the least. The splitting of white light into its colours is called dispersion. A glass prism on a desk does the same job in a bigger shape, and shows the fan of colours better than any raindrop.

![A triangular prism with a white beam entering one side and a fan of colours leaving the other](/media/prism-dispersion.png)
*A triangular prism takes a white beam on one side and sends a fan of colours out of the other, red bent least and violet most.*

The mirror part comes next. The light carries on through the drop, reflects once off the back of it, and leaves the drop, bending again on the way out. Between the two bends and the reflection, the ray turns about 138 degrees. That is what puts the bow about 42 degrees away from the point directly opposite the sun. Red, bent the least, sits on the outside of the bow, and violet, bent the most, sits on the inside. The whole bow is only about a degree and a quarter wide. Some rays reflect twice inside the drop instead of once. They make a second, fainter bow further out, at about 51 degrees, with its colours the other way round.

The sun has to be behind you for the bow to show, because you are seeing the drops that lie in the direction opposite it. The drops must also be about half a millimetre across to give a bright bow. Drops from fog are far too small, and they give a white halo instead.

# Medium

A rainbow is sunlight scattered by millions of water droplets. Each droplet acts as a prism and a mirror at once, and the bow is the result of those two jobs happening together in every drop along the line of sight.

## Two jobs in one drop

The prism part comes first. Light crosses the surface of the drop from air into water. It slows down, because water holds it back, and it bends towards the straight line through the surface. How much it bends depends on the colour. The index of water is about 1.343 for violet light and about 1.331 for red, so violet is bent the most and red the least. White light comes apart into a spectrum, and this splitting is called dispersion.

Then the mirror part. The light carries on through the drop to the far side, where a little of it reflects off the back surface and crosses the drop again. On the way out it speeds back up into the air, and it bends once more, away from the straight line. The light that reaches your eye has been bent on entering, reflected once inside, and bent again on leaving.

## The path through a drop

![A single raindrop in cross-section, with a ray entering, bending, reflecting once off the back of the drop and leaving, the angle of deviation marked](/media/rainbow-angle.png)
*One ray crosses a single raindrop, bends on entry, reflects once off the back of the drop, and leaves again, with the total angle of deviation marked.*

In the primary bow the total turn of the ray is about 138 degrees. Since the light started out travelling from the sun, that turn leaves the bow sitting about 42 degrees away from the point directly opposite the sun. Red, bent the least, ends up on the outside of the bow, and violet, bent the most, ends up on the inside. The whole bow is only about a degree and a quarter wide.

## The geometry of the bow

![An observer with the sun behind them, a cone of rays at about 42 degrees around the point opposite the sun, and the circular bow seen from above](/media/rainbow-geometry.png)
*Seen from above, the sun sits behind the observer, rays leave the drops in a cone about 42 degrees wide around the point opposite the sun, and the bow is a full circle centred on that point.*

Because the bow is centred on the point opposite the sun, it is a full circle. From the ground you see only the arc that is above the horizon. You can see the whole ring only from a high place or from an aircraft.

The sun has to be behind you for the bow to show at all. The drops that send light to your eye are the ones in front of you, and they are lit from behind by the sun, which is why the bow is always opposite the sun and never in the same part of the sky.

## The drops have to be the right size

The droplets must be about half a millimetre across to give the bright bow. A drop of that size spreads the returning light into the narrow band of colour you can see. Drops from fog are far too small for that, and they give a white halo instead.

## A second bow

The secondary bow comes from a ray that reflects twice inside the drop. It sits at about 51 degrees from the point opposite the sun, it is fainter than the primary bow, and its colours run the other way round, with red on the inside.

## The sky behind the bow

The light reaching a drop has already crossed the sky, and the same scattering that makes [the blue sky](/post/why-the-sky-is-blue) blue has taken some of the blue out of it. That is one reason the colours of a bow look so clean.

# Long

A rainbow is sunlight scattered by millions of water droplets. Each droplet works like a prism and a mirror at once. The prism splits the light into its colours, and the mirror sends the light back towards you. Nearly everything else about a rainbow follows from those two jobs and from where the sun has to be: the circle, the width of the band, the order of the colours, and the fainter second bow that usually sits outside the first.

## The bend inside a drop

Light travels through the air and then crosses into a drop of water. It slows down, because water holds it back, and it bends towards the straight line through the surface. How much it bends depends on the material it has entered and on the colour of the light. The number that measures this is called the index of refraction.

For water the index is about 1.343 for violet light and about 1.331 for red. Violet is bent the most and red the least. The difference is small, and it is the whole reason a rainbow has colours in it. White light comes apart into a spectrum, and that splitting is called dispersion. A raindrop is a prism in exactly the sense that a wedge of glass is a prism, only smaller and rounder.

The bend happens in the first layer of water the ray crosses, and then the ray keeps its new direction all the way through the drop. It is bent once at the surface, and it is not bent again on the way through, because the light is already moving at its new speed. The only other bending happens when the ray leaves the drop and the speed changes again.

## The mirror in the same drop

Not all the light that enters a drop comes back out the way it went in. At the back surface of the drop, some of it reflects and crosses the drop again. Some of that reflects a second time and crosses once more. The light that leaves after one reflection makes the primary bow. The light that leaves after two reflections makes the secondary bow.

On the way out of the drop, the light speeds back up into the air and bends once more, away from the straight line through the surface. So a ray in the primary bow is bent on entering, reflected once inside, and bent again on leaving. The total turn is about 138 degrees.

## The circle in the sky

A turn of about 138 degrees means the light arrives at your eye from a direction about 42 degrees away from the point directly opposite the sun. The sun is behind you and the drops are in front of you, so the bow is centred on that point.

Because the centre is fixed in the direction opposite the sun, the bow is a full circle. Every drop lying about 42 degrees from that point sends you light of the same colour, so the ring is complete all the way round. Standing on the ground, you see the upper part of it as an arc. The rest lies below the horizon or is hidden by the landscape. You can see the whole ring only from a high place or from an aircraft.

If you stand with your back to the sun, your own shadow points at the middle of the circle, and the bow surrounds the tip of it. That is the quickest way to find the centre of a bow without having to work out where the sun is.

The sun must be behind you. If the sun is in front, the light reaching your eye has not been turned round by a drop, and there is no bow. This is why a rainbow appears opposite the sun, low in the sky in the late afternoon and high in the sky around midday.

## The order of the colours

Red is bent the least and violet the most, so on the primary bow red lies on the outside and violet on the inside. The bow is not broad. The whole band is only about a degree and a quarter wide, which is about twice the width of the full moon.

The colours of the secondary bow run the other way, because the extra reflection changes which of the two bends does the most work.

The band is narrow because only a narrow range of angles sends a strong concentration of light back to you. Move the angle a little and the colours spread out and wash into each other. Across the band, red gives way to orange, yellow, green, blue and violet, in that order, from the outer edge inwards.

## Many drops make one bow

No single drop makes the bow you see. Your eye takes light from every drop lying about 42 degrees from the point opposite the sun, and each of those drops sends you light of a slightly different colour. The bow is the band where all of them together are strongest, and the colours arrive in the order the bending predicts.

This is why the bow has a sharp inner edge and a softer outer one, and why the colour at a given angle is the same everywhere round the circle. A drop a long way off and a drop close by both contribute to the same band of colour.

## Two bows

![Two arcs around the point opposite the sun, the primary bow at about 42 degrees and the secondary bow at about 51 degrees, the secondary with its colours the other way round](/media/rainbow-orders.png)
*Two arcs sit around the point opposite the sun: the primary bow at about 42 degrees, red outside, and the fainter secondary bow at about 51 degrees with its colours the other way round.*

The secondary bow comes from a ray that reflects twice inside the drop. The second reflection turns the ray further, so it leaves at about 51 degrees from the point opposite the sun rather than 42. It is fainter, because fewer rays take that path, and its colours are reversed, with red on the inside of the bow and violet on the outside.

Between the two bows there is a darker band. No rays from either bow arrive at that angle, so it reads as a gap in the colour.

## The size of the drops

The droplets must be about half a millimetre across to give the bright bow. At that size the returning light is spread into the narrow band of colour you can see, and the bow is bright and sharp. Drops from fog are far too small for that, and they give a white halo instead. Larger drops, the kind that come with heavy rain, make a wider and fainter bow.

## A bow made on purpose

Anyone can make one. A hose held with a fine spray, with the sun behind the person holding it, throws a small rainbow in the mist, low in the air, and the same rule holds there as in the sky: the drops must be about half a millimetre across, and the sun must be behind you. On a fine day the sun can be too high for a bow to fit into the sky at all, and you need a shower to bring it down.

## What is left of the sun

The light that lights the drops has already crossed the sky. Near the horizon, at sunrise and sunset, it has crossed a great deal of air, and the short blue wavelengths have been scattered out of it. That is why the light is red, and [a red sunset](/post/why-sunsets-are-red) is the same story told from the other side. A rainbow at the end of the day often borrows that red, and the outer band glows. The same loss of blue and green happens in the bow, so the colours of a low rainbow are deeper than the colours of one seen at midday.

## The short life of a bow

A bow needs two things at once: a sun behind you and rain in front. When the sun moves, or when the shower passes, the drops in front of you dry up or thin out, and the bow is gone. It lasts minutes, and no two are quite the same, because the drops that make it are always moving and always changing their size.

The rule is simple. Stand with your back to the sun, face the rain, and the sky does the rest.
