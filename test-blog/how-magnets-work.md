# Medium

Magnets stop being mysterious once you know what a magnetic field is. A moving electric charge makes a field, and every atom is full of moving electrons, so every piece of matter carries a field of its own. The whole of magnetisation is about what those tiny fields do when they line up.

## A moving charge makes a field

Push a current through a wire and a magnetic field curls around the wire. The same thing happens on a far bigger scale in [lightning and electric current](/post/why-thunder-follows-lightning), where a vast current of charge races down a cloud and makes a field of its own on the way.

## Domains inside a metal

In iron, nickel and cobalt the magnetic moments of neighbouring atoms line up in regions called domains, each one a patch of metal where the atoms lean the same way. In an unmagnetised bar magnet the domains point in different directions, so their fields cancel each other out and the bar shows no field outside itself.

Bring a magnet near and its field reaches into the bar and does two things: it makes the domains grow, and it makes them turn. Domains already leaning towards the new magnet grow at the expense of their neighbours, and domains pointing the wrong way shrink or turn over. After a while many of them point the same way, and the bar has a north end and a south end.

Take the magnet away and the new arrangement stays. Nothing is running, no current is flowing, and yet the bar keeps its field for years. That is what makes a magnet permanent.

## Heat and the limit of a permanent magnet

Heat gets in the way. Above about 770 degrees Celsius iron loses its ability to stay magnetised, because the heat shakes the domains apart and they point in all directions again. Cool the iron in a strong field and the domains line up once more as it cools, and the bar is magnetised afresh.

## The shape of the field

Outside a magnet the field runs from the north pole to the south pole, and inside the magnet it runs back from south to north. The lines therefore form closed loops that never begin or end anywhere. The drawing of lines looping round a bar magnet is only a picture, though. The lines are not real strings in the air, and nothing streams along them. The field itself is real, and the plainest proof is a compass: a compass needle is a small magnet, and it turns until it lies along the field.

The field also weakens quickly with distance, roughly as the inverse square of the distance. A magnet twice as far away is about a quarter as strong, and three times as far away about a ninth. This is why a magnet can lift a paperclip at one centimetre and cannot lift it across the room.

## Magnets you can switch on and off

An electromagnet is a coil of wire round a soft iron core. The field comes from the current in the coil, and the soft iron core makes it stronger and gives it a clear north and south end. Switch the current off and the field goes with it. This is what lets a crane lift scrap metal, a speaker push a cone and a motor turn a shaft.

# Long

Magnets are everywhere, and most of them are working quietly. A moving electric charge makes a field, and every atom is full of moving electrons, so every piece of matter carries a field of its own. The whole of magnetisation is about what those tiny fields do when they line up.

## A moving charge makes a field

Push a current through a wire and a magnetic field curls around the wire. Put a moving charge into a field and it feels a push sideways, which is how an electric motor turns current into movement. The rule has two halves and both are simple: moving charge makes a field, and a field pushes back on moving charge.

Now think how much is moving inside a single atom. Every atom holds electrons in motion, and those electrons are moving charges, so each atom makes a magnetic field of its own. Every piece of matter around you is quietly magnetic. The field of one atom is far too weak to feel, but matter is made of an unimaginable number of atoms, and when their fields agree they add together into something a hand can pick up.

The same rule accounts for the field that curls around the wire in your house, the field that makes a hard drive hold its data, and the field that guides a charged particle in a particle accelerator. None of them needs iron. Iron only makes the effect stronger and lasting.

## Domains, and the making of a permanent magnet

In most materials the magnetic moments of neighbouring atoms point in all directions and their fields cancel each other out. Iron, nickel and cobalt are the metals where that does not happen. In them the magnetic moments of neighbouring atoms line up in regions called domains, each one a patch of metal where the atoms lean the same way.

Take an unmagnetised bar of iron and look inside it. The domains are there, but they point in different directions, so they cancel each other out and the bar shows no field outside itself. It is not really unmagnetised. It is a crowd of small magnets pointing every which way.

Bring a real magnet near and its field does two things to the domains: it makes them grow, and it makes them turn. Domains that already leaned a little towards the new magnet grow at the expense of their neighbours, and domains pointing the wrong way shrink or flip over. After a while many of them point the same way, and the bar has a north end and a south end.

Take the magnet away and the domains stay as they are. That is the part that makes a magnet permanent. The new arrangement is held in place by the exchange of forces between neighbouring atoms, so nothing is running, no current is flowing, and yet the bar keeps its field for years.

Heat gets in the way of this. Above about 770 degrees Celsius iron passes what is called the Curie point and loses its ability to stay magnetised. The heat shakes the domains apart, so they point in all directions once more. Cool the iron in a strong field, though, and the domains line up again as it cools, and the bar is magnetised afresh.

## The loops around a bar magnet

Outside a magnet the field runs from the north pole to the south pole. Inside the magnet it runs the other way, from south back to north. Because a field never begins or ends, the lines have to join up: they leave the north pole, curve out through the air, and enter the south pole, then continue through the metal to the north pole again. Every line is a closed loop.

![Field lines looping out of the north pole of a bar magnet and back into the south pole](/media/field-lines.png#center)
*The lines leave the north pole, arc through the air around the bar and enter the south pole, and the arrows on them show the direction the field points along every part of the loop.*

This is the picture in every textbook, and it is worth saying plainly what it is. The lines are a drawing, not real strings hanging in the air. You cannot pick one up, and no substance streams along it. They are a way of showing the strength and the shape of a field: the number of lines tells you how strong the field is, and the spacing between them tells you how it changes from place to place.

The field itself is real, and the simplest proof is a compass. A compass needle is a small magnet, and it turns until it lies along the field. Put it near a bar magnet and it swings round to point from one pole towards the other.

## How the field falls away

The field weakens quickly as you move away from a magnet. Roughly, its strength follows the inverse square of the distance: twice as far away and it is about a quarter as strong, three times as far away and about a ninth. This is why a magnet can lift a paperclip at one centimetre and cannot lift it at ten. It is also why a magnet can hold a fridge door shut near its top and be powerless several metres away.

The same weakness makes small magnets useful in narrow places. A magnetised needle floating on a cork on water shows the point from the other side: it is a small magnet free to turn, so it simply swings into line with the Earth's field, which at that scale is far stronger than its own.

## Magnets you can switch on and off

An electromagnet is a coil of wire round a soft iron core. The field comes from the current in the coil, and the soft iron core gathers it up, makes it stronger and gives it a clear north and south end. Switch the current off and the field goes with it. There is nothing left to hold the domains in line, because soft iron has no reason to keep them.

That is the whole difference between a permanent magnet and an electromagnet, and between a magnet you have to carry around and one you can ask for when you want it. The strength of an electromagnet can be changed as easily as the current: more current, more field. It is how a crane lifts scrap metal, how a speaker pushes a cone, how a motor turns a shaft, and how a relay in an old telephone exchange connected one wire to another.

## The dynamo under our feet

The Earth is the largest magnet we know of, and it makes its field in much the same way. The outer core is a churning layer of liquid metal, mostly iron, that is neither solid nor still. The spin of the planet drags that metal with it, and the moving conducting fluid stirs up electric currents inside it. Those currents make a magnetic field, and the field then pushes back on the currents that made it. A moving conductor that keeps making its own field is a dynamo, and the core of the Earth is one.

The field has reversed many times over the history of the planet, on average every few hundred thousand years. The poles swap over, so what was the magnetic north becomes the magnetic south and back again. The changes have usually been gradual, with the pole wandering across the globe for a while, though at times the field has moved in a rush.

Nothing about [the seasons and the Earth's tilt](/post/why-we-have-seasons) comes from magnetism. The tilt of the axis and the orbit around the Sun decide those, not the churning core. The Earth's field is a separate story, though it comes from the same spinning, tilted, liquid-cored planet.

Today the Earth's magnetic pole that sits near the geographic south pole draws one end of a compass needle, while the pole near the geographic north draws the other. The north-seeking end of the needle points north, which is why a compass finds north.
