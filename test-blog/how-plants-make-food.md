# How Plants Make Food

# Sentence

Green leaves catch light, and with it they join carbon dioxide from the air and water from the soil to build sugar, which feeds the plant and gives out the oxygen we breathe.

# Paragraph

Leaves are the kitchen of a plant. Inside them, chlorophyll catches sunlight. That light energy drives a reaction which takes six molecules of carbon dioxide, drifting in through tiny pores called stomata, and six of water, drawn up from the soil by the roots. The result is one molecule of glucose, a sugar, and six of oxygen, which escapes through the same pores. The sugar is the plant's food. It builds new leaves, stems, roots, flowers and seeds. Without this work there would be no forests, no fields and no animal life, because plants stand at the base of every food chain.

# Short

A plant is not a passive thing sitting in the soil. It runs a factory, and the factory runs on sunlight. Inside every green leaf sit tiny packets called chloroplasts, and each one holds a green pigment called chlorophyll. Leaves are the working part of the plant, and the roots are the supply line that keeps them watered. The chlorophyll absorbs light, and that light pays for the whole business. What the plant makes is sugar. The sugar builds new leaves, stems, roots, flowers and seeds, and some of it is stored as starch in tubers and roots. That is why a potato feeds us as well as the plant.

The overall reaction is simple enough to write on a line. Six molecules of carbon dioxide and six of water, with light as the energy, give one molecule of glucose and six of oxygen. The carbon dioxide comes from the air and enters the leaf through pores on the underside called stomata. The water comes from the soil, pulled up through the roots and along the veins. The oxygen, which every animal needs, leaves by the same door. The plant takes in simple stuff from air and soil, and it gives back food and fresh air.

![A leaf with arrows showing carbon dioxide and water going in and sugar and oxygen coming out](/media/photosynthesis-overview.png)
*Two arrows point into the leaf: carbon dioxide from the air and water from the soil, arriving together with the light. Two arrows point out again: sugar, the food the plant stores, and oxygen, the gas that leaves for the air.*

Almost all the oxygen in the air was made this way. A good share of it comes not from trees but from drifting plants in the sea, which do the same work in the water. Plants breathe as well, day and night, taking in oxygen and giving out carbon dioxide. Sugar-making runs on light, while breathing runs all the time. A leaf is doing both at once, and the green colour is the visible sign of the machine at work. Hold a leaf up to the sun and you are looking at the front door of a food factory.

# Medium

Photosynthesis is the making of sugar from carbon dioxide and water with the energy of light. The overall reaction is short: six carbon dioxide plus six water plus light gives one glucose and six oxygen. Almost everything hard about it lies in the detail, and the detail happens inside the chloroplast, the small packet inside each leaf cell where the green pigment chlorophyll sits.

## Where the work happens

The light reactions take place in the thylakoid membranes, the flat sacs stacked inside the chloroplast. Here photosystem two splits water and sets free the oxygen we breathe. That splitting is the only large source of free oxygen on Earth. The energy in the light is captured and banked, in the form of two carrier molecules, ATP and NADPH.

The second stage is the Calvin cycle. It runs in the fluid of the stroma, the liquid that fills the rest of the chloroplast. Here the ATP and NADPH are spent, and carbon dioxide is fixed onto a sugar called RuBP. The enzyme that does the fixing is RuBisCO. RuBisCO is slow. It can manage only a few molecules of carbon dioxide each second, which is why a leaf has to carry so many chloroplasts, and why a plant keeps so much of its bulk in green leaf.

## Why leaves are green

Chlorophyll does not take in all light equally. It absorbs blue light strongly, near 430 to 450 nanometres, and red light strongly as well, near 640 to 680. In the middle of the visible range, around 550 nanometres, the green light is mostly reflected rather than absorbed. Green light is what reaches your eye, and that is why leaves look green. An autumn leaf turns yellow and red because the chlorophyll has broken down and the pigments underneath it are showing.

*Two curves are drawn against the wavelength of the light. The chlorophyll a curve and the chlorophyll b curve both climb steeply in the blue, fall away to their lowest point through the green middle, and rise again in the red. Neither curve takes much of the green.*

## The doors and the water

The stomata open to let carbon dioxide in, and water vapour goes out through them at the same time. A plant can lose a great deal of water this way, which is why it needs wet ground and deep roots. Water is not an ordinary liquid. When it freezes it expands instead of shrinking, and the ice can burst the fine plumbing inside a leaf and kill it. [Water and ice](/post/why-ice-floats-on-water) explains why.

## Two ways around the problem

Some plants have found ways to make the slow business of photosynthesis cheaper. Maize and sugarcane concentrate carbon dioxide around RuBisCO first, so the sluggish enzyme always has plenty to work with. Pineapples and cacti open their stomata at night instead, taking in carbon dioxide when the air is cool and damp, and shutting them by day to hold on to the water.

## Plants breathe as well

Plants also respire, taking in oxygen and giving out carbon dioxide, day and night. Respiration is the reverse set of reactions, and it releases the energy stored in sugar for the plant to use. A plant makes sugar by day with light, and it spends that sugar around the clock. Almost all the oxygen in the air was made by photosynthesis, and a good share of it comes from drifting plants in the sea rather than from trees.

# Long

Plants do something no animal can do. They take in air, water and light, and they build their own food out of the raw material. The process is called photosynthesis, and it has been running in the seas and on the land for billions of years. Almost every meal you have ever eaten began here, because plants sit at the base of nearly every food chain. The overall reaction is short enough to write down: six molecules of carbon dioxide plus six molecules of water plus light give one molecule of glucose and six molecules of oxygen. The glucose is the sugar that feeds the plant, and the oxygen is the gas that feeds us. All of it happens inside leaves.

## The leaf is a stack of layers

A leaf is not a flat green sheet. Cut one across and you find a set of layers, each with its own job.

The top is the upper epidermis, a clear, waxy skin that lets light in but holds water back. Under it sits the palisade layer, a tight row of tall cells packed with chloroplasts. They stand shoulder to shoulder, in the light, doing most of the work. Below that is the spongy layer, a loose tangle of cells with wide air spaces between them. Those spaces let carbon dioxide spread through the leaf and reach the cells that need it. Threaded through the layers run the veins, the pipes that carry water in from the roots and sugar out to the rest of the plant. The bottom is the lower epidermis, and set into it are the stomata, each one a pore flanked by two guard cells that open and shut it.

## The first stage: catching light

All of this chemistry happens inside the chloroplasts, the small bodies that pack the leaf cells. The first stage runs in the thylakoid membranes, the flattened sacs stacked inside the chloroplast.

Here photosystem two does the crucial work. It splits water, and that splitting releases the oxygen we breathe. It also takes the energy in the light and passes it on. This stage has no carbon dioxide in it at all. It is purely about light, water and the building of energy stores.

Those energy stores come as two molecules: ATP and NADPH. ATP is the currency the cell spends on work, and NADPH carries the raw material for building sugar. Both are made in the light reactions, and both are handed to the next stage. The oxygen meanwhile simply leaves. It goes out through the stomata and into the air, and it is the reason there is oxygen to breathe at all.

## The second stage: building sugar

The second stage is the Calvin cycle. It runs in the stroma, the fluid that fills the chloroplast around the thylakoids, and it is where the carbon dioxide finally gets locked into sugar.

The carbon dioxide is fixed onto a sugar called RuBP. The enzyme that does the fixing is RuBisCO, and RuBisCO is slow. On a good day it manages a few molecules of carbon dioxide each second, while the light reactions feed it far more energy than it can use. That gap is the reason a leaf carries so many chloroplasts, packed as densely as they can be.

The ATP and NADPH from the light reactions are spent here. The energy they carry drives the reactions, and the NADPH supplies the hydrogen and the electrons that the new sugar needs. What leaves the cycle is not sugar itself but three-carbon pieces, which are sent out to be built up into glucose and then into starch, cellulose and everything else a plant is made of. A sugar molecule of six carbons needs six molecules of carbon dioxide, and each turn of the cycle takes in one of them.

## Why a leaf is green

The colour comes from the chlorophyll itself. Chlorophyll does not take in light equally across the spectrum. It absorbs blue light strongly, near 430 to 450 nanometres, and it absorbs red light strongly as well, near 640 to 680 nanometres. The light in the middle, the green around 550 nanometres, is mostly thrown back out rather than absorbed.

A green leaf is therefore a leaf that is reflecting green at you. Change the pigment and you change the colour. Leaves that are red or purple carry other pigments that take in the green and pass the red. When autumn comes, the chlorophyll breaks down first, and the yellows and oranges underneath it are revealed.

## The doors in the leaf

Everything that enters or leaves the leaf passes through the stomata. Open, they let carbon dioxide in from the air and let water vapour out. The vapour leaving is transpiration, and a plant can lose a lot of water this way. On a hot dry day the stomata partly close, and the plant trades growth for survival.

Each pore is flanked by two guard cells. When they swell with water they bow apart and the pore opens. When they lose water they come together and it shuts. That is a piece of engineering in miniature, a valve the plant can work without any nervous system or muscle.

The water that leaves as vapour came up from the roots, and the water that goes in again comes back the same way. The veins are the pipework. Xylem carries water up towards the leaves, and phloem carries the sugar away to roots, fruits and growing buds. A leaf is the point where the plumbing of the plant meets the sky.

## Two clever tricks

Some plants have found ways to make the slow business of photosynthesis cheaper.

Maize and sugarcane, along with other grasses, concentrate carbon dioxide around RuBisCO before the Calvin cycle begins. They use extra machinery in the leaf, extra pumps that cost energy, but the saving is large, because RuBisCO then works on a gas that is many times richer in carbon dioxide than ordinary air. The cost is worth it in hot, bright places where the plant would otherwise lose too much water.

Pineapples and cacti take a different route. They open their stomata at night, when the air is cool and damp, take in carbon dioxide and store it as an acid in the cells. By day the stomata are shut, and the plant uses its store, so it trades a little extra chemical work for a large saving of water. This is called CAM photosynthesis, after the family Crassulaceae in which it was first noticed.

## Plants breathe too

Plants also respire, all the time. Respiration takes in oxygen and gives out carbon dioxide, and it happens day and night.

The two processes look like mirror images, but they are not the same thing. Photosynthesis stores energy in sugar, and it only works when there is light. Respiration breaks sugar down again and releases that energy, so the plant can build new tissue, repair damage and move water around. A plant makes sugar by day with light, and it spends that sugar around the clock. It is also why a plant still breathes in the dark, in a shed, with no light at all.

## The air we breathe

Almost all the oxygen in the air was made by photosynthesis. Not all of it by trees. A good share comes from the sea, from drifting plants that float in the water and do the same work in their own small packets of chloroplast.

This matters because it means the ocean has been a factory for the atmosphere for a very long time, and it means that clearing the sea would take oxygen away from us. On land the trees and grass do their part. Between them, the plants of the sea and the plants of the land keep the air at the level of oxygen that animals, including us, need.

## Light through the year

Everything above depends on how much light falls on a leaf, and that changes with the seasons. Over the year the length of the day moves, and with it the hours a plant has to gather light. [The seasons](/post/why-we-have-seasons) explains why.

A plant in a dark winter month is not working as hard as the same plant in June. Growth slows. Some plants drop their leaves altogether and rely on stored sugar in the roots until spring. Others keep green leaves and take whatever light there is.

At the other end of the scale, plants in bright sun have their own problem. Too much light can damage the photosystems, so a plant shuts down some of its light reactions and spends the surplus on other chemistry. Even the simplest factory needs to know when to slow down.
