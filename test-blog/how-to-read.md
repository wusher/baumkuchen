# How To Read This Blog

# Sentence

Every article is written five times over, from one sentence to three pages. Use the slider to choose how deep you read, and the site remembers your choice for the next article.

# Paragraph

Every article is written five times over: a sentence, a paragraph, three paragraphs, a page, and three pages. The slider under the title chooses between them, and the site remembers the depth you picked, so the next article opens at the same depth. The left and right arrow keys work from anywhere on the page, and a link that ends in a depth name takes you straight to that depth.

# Short

Every article in this blog is written five times over: once as a single sentence, once as a single paragraph, once as three paragraphs, once as a page, and once as three pages. Nothing is missing and nothing is repeated — the words are the same facts, told at the length that suits the moment.

The slider under the title moves between them. Drag it, click along it, or use the ‹ and › buttons at either end. The label under the slider tells you where you are and how long that depth takes to read.

You do not have to touch it. The site remembers the depth you last chose and opens the next article at the same place, so a reader who wants the whole thing can read one thing. A link that ends in a depth name beats both: it takes you to that depth on purpose, whichever one you read last.

# Medium

A blog that writes every article five times is unusual, so here is how it works.

## The five depths

The five depths are named for the shape of the text, not for the difficulty. **Sentence** is one sentence, at most thirty-five words, and it is the whole idea. **Paragraph** is one paragraph of a hundred words or so. **Short** is three paragraphs. **Medium** is about a page. **Long** is three pages, with headings.

Every article here carries all five, so the slider has all five stops on every article. Nothing is a summary of something else: each depth is written out in full, in the plainest words the writer could manage.

## Moving between the depths

Four ways, and they all do the same move. The slider can be dragged or clicked. The ‹ and › buttons step one depth at a time. The left and right arrow keys work from anywhere on the page, as long as you are not typing in a field. And a link that ends in a depth name — say `/post/why-the-sky-is-blue#medium` — takes you to that depth.

The arrow keys are safe to take, because nothing on the page scrolls sideways. Up, down, home and end work as well, but only while the slider itself has the focus, so that a reader scrolling the page never loses the text they were reading.

## What the site remembers

The depth you choose is kept in the browser, and the next article opens there. That is a convenience, not a rule: move the slider once and every article after it starts at the same place, until you move it again.

A link beats both the article and the reader. If someone sends you a link to a depth, you land on that depth even if you have been reading the long ones all week.

# Long

Most blogs make you choose before you start. This one makes you choose after you start, and lets you keep changing your mind.

## One article, five lengths

Each of the ten articles is written out five times. The sentence version is the idea and nothing else; the paragraph version adds the one thing a reader needs to make sense of it; the short version gives the shape of the argument; the medium version fills it in; and the long version adds the detail and the headings that make it readable at a sitting.

The five are not excerpts of one another and they are not a summary with padding. Each is written to be read on its own, which is why the words differ from one depth to the next even when the facts do not. A reader who stops at the sentence has not been short-changed, and a reader who reads the long depth has not been served a summary of it.

## The slider

The control under the title is a plain range input, so it works with a mouse, a finger and a keyboard. Drag it and the text changes as you move; click a point on it and it jumps there. The ‹ and › buttons at either end step one stop at a time, and each one carries a label for screen readers, because an arrow on its own tells a blind reader nothing.

Under the slider sits a line telling you the name of the current depth and how long it takes to read: the word count and the time at a usual reading speed. The numbers come from the text itself, so they are honest; a picture and its caption are not counted, because a reader does not read them as prose.

## The keyboard

The left and right arrow keys move between the depths from anywhere on the page. Typing in a field is never interrupted, and the keys are only taken when the page is not asking for something else.

The up and down keys, and home and end, work as well, but only while the slider has the focus. That is deliberate: a reader who is scrolling with the arrows should not have the text change under them, and a reader who has reached for the slider should be able to walk it without leaving the keyboard.

## Addresses that carry the depth

The depth is part of the address. An article lives at `/post/why-the-sky-is-blue`, and the long version of it at `/post/why-the-sky-is-blue#long`. Moving the slider writes the name into the address as you go, so the address of what you are reading can always be copied and sent to someone else.

Three things can say which depth an article opens at, and they rank in a fixed order. The address comes first, because a link someone made on purpose should be honoured. The article comes second, if it names a depth of its own in its front matter. The reader comes last, in the depth the browser has kept from last time.

## If you would rather not read at all

You are allowed to leave at the first line. The sentence depth is a complete answer to the question in the title, and on a good day it is the whole article in one breath.
