A hot air balloon rises because hot air inside is lighter than the cool air around it, so it pushes aside more weight of air than it weighs, and the surplus lifts it up.

# Medium

A hot air balloon rises because the air inside it is lighter than the air outside it. That is the whole of it, and it follows from a rule that is much older than balloons.

## The rule of Archimedes

Archimedes found that anything sitting in a fluid is pushed upwards by the weight of the fluid it pushes aside. Hold a block of wood under water and the water pushes back until the block floats or sinks on its own terms. A balloon does the same thing with air. It pushes aside a great column of cool air, and that cool air weighs more than the balloon, the basket and everyone in it, so the surplus pushes the balloon up.

## Why hot air is lighter

The same gas weighs less when it is warmer. At the same pressure, the density of air falls as the temperature rises, because the molecules sit further apart and each cubic metre holds less matter. Air at 90 degrees Celsius weighs about 0.97 kilograms per cubic metre, while air at 15 degrees weighs about 1.225. The same idea sits behind [density in ice and water](/post/why-ice-floats-on-water), where what decides the answer is not the substance but how tightly it is packed.

## The arithmetic of a sport balloon

A sport balloon holds about 2,500 cubic metres of air. It pushes aside about 3,000 kilograms of cool air, while the hot air inside weighs about 2,400 kilograms. That leaves roughly 600 kilograms of lift, and it has to carry the envelope, the basket, the burners, the fuel and the people aboard.

The pilot manages this with the burner, which heats the air and changes the temperature whenever they want to climb or settle. A bigger envelope gives more lift, and to lift more the air inside has to be hotter. The envelope is made of a light, strong fabric so that it does not eat the lift it is meant to provide, and the basket is kept small for the same reason. Every kilogram of metal and canvas aboard is a kilogram of lift given away.

## The two forces on a balloon

The upward push comes from the cool air outside, and its size depends on the density of that air and how much of it the balloon displaces. The downward pull is the balloon's own weight: the envelope, the basket, the burners, the fuel, the hot air inside and the crew. When the first is larger than the second, the balloon goes up. When they match, it hangs still.

## Cold mornings are best

Balloons fly best on a cool morning, when the ground is cold and the air close to it is denser. A denser surrounding air means more weight pushed aside, and more lift for the same envelope. As the day warms, the air is stirred and thinned, so the balloon settles towards the ground again as evening comes on.

## The ceiling

The pressure falls with height, so the air grows thinner as you climb. At 3,000 metres the air holds about 70 per cent of the weight it holds at sea level, and a balloon has a ceiling. The same volume of cool air up high simply does not weigh enough to hold up the load.

## Drifting, not steering

A balloon cannot steer. It drifts with the wind, and the pilot changes height to find a layer of air moving the right way. The wind usually travels in layers that do not all blow the same direction, so climbing or descending lets the balloon join a layer heading where the pilot wants to go. The route is chosen by the sky rather than by the crew.

# Long

A hot air balloon rises because the air inside it is lighter than the air outside it. Once you understand why hot air is lighter, and how much weight a balloon can push aside, the flight stops being a mystery and becomes arithmetic.

## The rule of Archimedes

The principle behind every balloon was worked out in the third century BC by Archimedes, and it is short enough to say in one sentence. Anything in a fluid is pushed up by the weight of the fluid it pushes aside.

That covers ships, divers, icebergs and balloons alike. Hold a block of wood under water and the water pushes back on it. The block rises until the weight of the water it has displaced matches its own weight, and then it floats there. If you push it under, it wants to come back up.

A hot air balloon does the same trick, with air as the fluid. The envelope is a great bag that pushes aside a large volume of the surrounding air. If that volume of cool air weighs more than the balloon plus everything carried in it, the balloon rises. The difference between the two weights is called the lift, and a balloon flies on the surplus.

## Why hot air is lighter

Air does not change when you heat it. It is the same gas, the same molecules, mostly nitrogen and oxygen. What changes is how tightly the molecules are packed.

At a given pressure, a cubic metre of gas holds a fixed number of molecules. Heat them and they move faster and push apart, so the gas spreads out and thins. Density, the mass in each cubic metre, therefore falls as the temperature rises.

The numbers are worth holding on to. Air at 15 degrees Celsius weighs about 1.225 kilograms for every cubic metre. Air at 90 degrees weighs about 0.97 kilograms for the same cubic metre. That is the whole trick in two figures: the same space, holding rather less than the weight it held before.

A useful way to picture it is to imagine a room full of tightly packed marbles and then the same room with the marbles spread out to twice the size. The marbles have not changed, but the room now weighs less per cubic metre. The same idea governs why ice floats on water rather than sinking, and what decides the answer there is the packing of the substance rather than what it is made of.

## The arithmetic of a sport balloon

A sport balloon holds about 2,500 cubic metres of air inside its envelope. Fill that volume with cool air at 15 degrees and it would weigh about 3,000 kilograms, which is roughly the weight of the surrounding air the balloon pushes aside.

Now heat it. The burner raises the temperature of the air inside, the air thins, and the same 2,500 cubic metres weigh about 2,400 kilograms instead. The gap between the two figures, roughly 600 kilograms, is the lift available.

That lift has to carry everything: the envelope, the basket, the burners, the fuel and the people aboard. The margin is not generous, which is why every part of a balloon is built as lightly as it can be. The envelope is made of a light, strong fabric so that it does not eat the lift it is meant to provide, and the basket is kept small for the same reason. Burners and their fuel take up weight too, and every kilogram of that is a kilogram of lift handed back to the sky.

## The pilot and the burner

The burner is the only control the pilot has over the physics. It sits in the mouth of the envelope and heats the air that fills it.

Turn the burner up and the air inside gets hotter, the density falls further, and the balloon gains lift and climbs. Turn it down and the air cools, the envelope shrinks slightly, the density rises, and the balloon settles back down. There is no wheel and no rudder. Height is the only dial.

A bigger envelope gives more lift, because there is more volume to push aside and more air inside to make light. To lift more still, the air inside has to be hotter. Both knobs turn the same way: more of everything means more lift.

## The two forces on a balloon

![Two forces acting on a hot air balloon](/media/buoyancy-forces.png)
*The arrow pointing upwards is the weight of the cool air the balloon pushes aside. The arrow pointing downwards is the weight of the balloon itself, with the hot air inside it.*

The two arrows in that picture are the whole argument. The upward arrow is the push from the cool air outside, and the length of it depends on the density of that air and how much of it the balloon displaces. The downward arrow is the balloon's own weight, made up of the envelope, the basket, the burners, the fuel, the hot air inside and the crew. When the first arrow is longer than the second, the balloon goes up. When they match, the balloon hangs still. When the second wins, it comes down.

## Cold mornings are best

Balloons fly best on a cool morning, when the ground is cold and the air close to it is denser. A denser surrounding air means more weight pushed aside, and more lift for the same envelope. On a warm afternoon the air has been stirred and thinned, so the balloon works harder for less gain and tends to settle lower. As the ground cools again towards evening, the lift returns and the balloon can climb once more.

## A ceiling overhead

The pressure falls with height, so the air grows thinner as you climb. At 3,000 metres the air holds about 70 per cent of the weight it holds at sea level. A balloon that has been pushing aside cool, dense air near the ground finds far less to push aside up high, and the hot air inside it is not enough to make up the difference. That is why a balloon has a ceiling, and why the highest a balloon can climb depends on how heavy the day was.

## Drifting, not steering

A balloon cannot steer. It drifts with the wind, and the pilot changes height to find a layer of air moving the right way. The wind usually travels in layers that do not all blow the same direction, so climbing or descending lets the balloon join a layer heading the way the pilot wants to go. The route is chosen by the sky rather than by the crew.

It also means the balloon has to keep out of weather it cannot handle. Thunderstorms grow in exactly the conditions that make ballooning pleasant, with strong rising and falling currents, and the connection between those currents and the storms themselves is explained in [weather and thunderstorms](/post/why-thunder-follows-lightning). A balloon has no engine to outrun a storm and no way to steer around one, so the sensible pilot is on the ground when the sky starts to show off.

## The whole story in short

Archimedes' rule says anything in a fluid is pushed up by the weight of the fluid it pushes aside. Hot air is less dense than cool air, because heating spreads the same molecules further apart. A sport balloon of 2,500 cubic metres pushes aside about 3,000 kilograms of cool air while holding about 2,400 kilograms of hot air, leaving roughly 600 kilograms of lift. The burner sets the temperature, the light envelope and small basket keep the weight down, cool mornings give the densest air to push aside, thin air at 3,000 metres sets the ceiling, and the wind decides the route.
