# Why Ice Floats on Water

# Sentence

Water is densest at 4 degrees Celsius, so ice is lighter than the liquid around it and floats; the reason is the bent shape of the molecule, which locks neighbours into an open lattice.

# Paragraph

Ice floats because water is unusual. For nearly every other substance, the solid form is denser than the liquid, so it sinks. Water does the opposite, and the reason is the shape of its molecule. A water molecule has a bent form, and hydrogen bonds lock each molecule to four neighbours in an open hexagonal lattice. That lattice has gaps in it, so ice takes up more room than the same weight of liquid water. Ice weighs about 0.917 grams per cubic centimetre and liquid water about 1.000, so roughly a tenth of a floating block of ice stands above the surface.

# Short

Water behaves oddly when it freezes. Drop a lump of most solids into water and it sinks, because the solid form of a substance is nearly always denser than the liquid form. Put an ice cube in a glass and it floats to the top instead. The same goes for an iceberg, and for the skin of ice on a pond on a cold morning.

The explanation starts with the shape of the water molecule. It is bent rather than straight, and that bent shape lets the molecules hold on to each other by hydrogen bonds. Each molecule locks to four neighbours in a loose, open hexagonal lattice, and the gaps in that lattice leave more room around every molecule than there is in liquid water. Warm the ice and the lattice collapses. The molecules slide past each other and pack more tightly, so liquid water is denser than the ice that melted into it.

The numbers are plain. Ice weighs about 0.917 grams for each cubic centimetre. Liquid water weighs 1.000. That difference of nearly a tenth means that about a tenth of a floating block of ice stands above the waterline, which is why a floating cube looks as though it is resting on the surface rather than sitting in it. The same expansion causes real damage. Water that freezes inside a pipe pushes outward until the pipe bursts, and a full bottle left in a freezer can split.

In a lake, the same behaviour keeps life alive through the winter. Cold water is lighter than warm water, so it stays on top and the top layer freezes first. Ice is a poor conductor of heat, because the molecules in the lattice cannot move about to carry warmth away. The lid of ice slows the loss of heat from below, and the water at the bottom stays near 4 degrees, close to its density maximum. Fish and plants survive down there in the cold.

# Medium

Put an ice cube in a glass of water and it stays on top. Most people take this for granted, and so do most solid objects in a glass of water, because a solid is usually denser than the liquid around it and sinks. Water is one of the rare exceptions, and the reason for it is the shape of a single molecule.

A water molecule is bent, with two hydrogen atoms held at an angle to one oxygen atom. That bend lets the positive hydrogen atoms of one molecule reach towards the negative oxygen of another and grip it. These grips are hydrogen bonds. In ice, every molecule holds four neighbours this way, and the neighbours sit at the corners of a hexagon. The result is a tidy open lattice, a set of hexagonal rings with a gap in the middle of each one.

![The density of water against temperature](/media/water-density-curve.png#center)
*The curve climbs to a maximum at about 4 degrees Celsius, then falls away again towards freezing, where a marker shows ice at 0 degrees.*

Because that lattice has gaps, the molecules in ice sit further apart than the same molecules do in liquid water. When the ice melts, the lattice falls apart and the molecules pack in more closely. So the solid form of water ends up lighter than its liquid form, and the solid floats.

The curve shows the whole story. Water is densest at about 4 degrees Celsius. Water colder than that is lighter, and so is water that is warmer. Ice at 0 degrees is lighter again, at about 0.917 grams per cubic centimetre, while liquid water at 4 degrees comes to 1.000. Put those numbers together and about a tenth of a floating block of ice stands above the surface.

A floating cube in a glass is therefore mostly hidden. Only a thin cap of it stands proud of the waterline, and that is why ice looks as though it rests on the surface rather than sitting in it. The same holds for a floe on a lake or an iceberg in the sea: much of the block lies below, and only the ridge above the water can be seen.

That tenth matters in ordinary life. Water expands as it freezes, so a pipe full of water can burst when it freezes inside, and a full bottle left in a freezer can split. The expansion is the same one that keeps an ice cube afloat.

Lakes show the same behaviour in reverse. Cold water is lighter, so it stays on top of the warmer water below and freezes first. Ice is a poor conductor of heat, because the molecules held in the lattice cannot move about to carry warmth away. The lid of ice therefore slows the loss of heat from the water beneath it, and the water at the bottom of a lake stays near 4 degrees, close to its density maximum, through the coldest nights. Life survives down there.

The same principle turns up wherever density is the deciding factor. Density is simply weight packed into a given volume, and it settles whether a thing rises, sinks or stays where it is. A balloon rises because the warm air inside weighs less than the cooler air outside it, and that is the [density in a hot air balloon](/post/why-hot-air-balloons-rise) at work. A lake stratifies because cold water weighs less than warm water. In both cases the lighter material stays up and the heavier material settles below.

# Long

## The puzzle

Drop an ice cube into a glass of water and it stops at the surface, with a slice of it showing above the line. Set a big block of ice in a lake and the lake seems to hold it up. Most solids do the opposite. A lump of iron, a stone, a sugar cube dropped into water all go to the bottom without hesitating, because the solid form of those substances is denser than the liquid form.

Water is the exception, and the exception is large. Ice is about 92 per cent as dense as the water around it, which is why roughly a tenth of a floating block sits above the surface. One property of one common liquid decides whether a lake freezes from the top, whether a bottle splits in a freezer, and whether fish survive a northern winter.

## The bent molecule

Everything starts with the shape of a single water molecule. It is built from one oxygen atom and two hydrogen atoms, and it is not a straight line. The oxygen pulls harder on the shared electrons than the hydrogens do, and the two hydrogens are pushed to one side, so the molecule ends up bent, like a shallow V, with an angle of about 104 degrees between the hydrogens.

That bend does real work. It leaves the molecule polar, with a slight negative charge on the oxygen side and a slight positive charge on the hydrogen side. Because a bent molecule can put its positive end near a neighbour's negative end, the molecules reach towards each other and grip. That grip is a hydrogen bond.

Hydrogen bonds are much weaker than the bonds inside a molecule, and they break and reform constantly in liquid water. But when the temperature falls far enough, they hold the molecules in a fixed pattern, and that pattern is the whole of the trick.

## The open lattice

In ice, every molecule grips four neighbours and sits at the centre of a rough hexagon of them. Each of those neighbours grips four more, and the pattern runs through the whole solid as an open hexagonal lattice.

![Two packings of the same water molecules](/media/ice-lattice.png)
*On the left the molecules sit in an open hexagonal lattice with clear gaps between them; on the right the same molecules are disordered and packed closer together as a liquid.*

Look at the gaps in the left panel. In the lattice each molecule has room around it, held at arm's length by its neighbours and never closer than the hydrogen bonds allow. In the liquid on the right the lattice has gone. The molecules still touch and still form and break hydrogen bonds, but they can now slide past each other and drop into the gaps and corners that the rigid pattern could not use. The same molecules, then, take up less room.

That is the opposite of the usual rule. In most substances the particles pack more tightly in the solid than in the liquid, so the solid sinks and the liquid floats on it. Here the solid has the looser packing, so it floats and the liquid sits below it.

Carbon dioxide makes a fair comparison. Solid carbon dioxide, sold as dry ice, is denser than the gas around it, and a lump of it sinks through water and trails a white mist as it goes. That is the ordinary rule at work, and water is the exception to it.

## The numbers

The difference is easy to measure. Ice at 0 degrees Celsius weighs about 0.917 grams for each cubic centimetre. Liquid water at 4 degrees weighs 1.000. So the same weight of water takes up roughly a ninth more room when it is frozen.

That is where the tenth comes from. A floating block has to push aside its own weight of water, and since it is about 92 per cent as dense as that water, it settles until about 92 per cent of it lies under the surface. The remaining slice, a little under a tenth, rides on top. A cube of ice in a glass is therefore mostly hidden, which is why it looks as though it rests on the surface rather than in it.

The same idea explains a second oddity. Liquid water is densest at about 4 degrees Celsius. Water colder than that is lighter, and water warmer than that is lighter too. Only the narrow band around 4 degrees is at its heaviest.

## Cold water on top

That single fact decides how a lake freezes. A lake loses heat to the air from its surface. The surface water cools, and cold water is lighter than warm water, so it stays where it is rather than sinking. The layer at the top reaches 0 degrees and freezes, while the water below stays liquid.

If cold water sank, the freezing would run the other way, from the bottom up, and a lake would freeze solid. It does not, and the difference matters.

## A lid on the lake

The ice that forms is a poor conductor of heat, because the molecules in the lattice cannot move freely to carry warmth away from a warm surface and down into the cold. Warmth has to crawl along the lattice instead, and the lattice is a poor road for it.

So the layer of ice slows the loss of heat from the water beneath it. The deeper water never freezes. It settles near 4 degrees, close to its density maximum, in a layer under the ice, and it stays there through the coldest nights of the year.

This is why the ice on a pond grows downwards from the surface, and why a frozen pond can carry a foot on it while the water underneath stays liquid. The ice that forms is the lid, and the lid works because it is a bad conductor.

Under the ice, light still filters down through the water. Green plants keep working in the dim, taking in carbon dioxide and making food with the help of light. That is [how plants make food](/post/how-plants-make-food), and it means the bottom of a frozen lake is a refuge rather than a dead place. Fish, insects and weed all live there, in water that is barely above freezing but never quite frozen.

## The expansion that splits things

The same expansion is not always convenient. When water freezes it grows by about a ninth in volume, and it grows at the moment it locks into the lattice, when it has the least room to give. Water inside a pipe presses outward on all sides at once, and a pipe already stiff with age can split. A bottle filled to the neck and left in a freezer can do the same, which is why a partly filled bottle survives better than a full one.

Rock is less forgiving. Water that enters a crack and freezes there wedges it open, one of the ways that cliffs wear back and that paving stones are broken up over a winter.

## The exception worth keeping

Most substances behave the other way round: the solid is denser than the liquid, so it sinks. Water does not, and the reason sits at the level of a single bent molecule and the open lattice its hydrogen bonds build. Everything else in the story follows from that shape: the tenth of an ice cube that stands above the water, the pipe that bursts, the bottle that splits, the lake that freezes from the top, and the cold clear water at the bottom where the fish wait out the winter.
