# Why Sunsets Are Red

# Sentence

A sunset is red because the sunlight has crossed far more air on its way to your eye, and that air scattered the blue out of the beam, leaving red and orange behind.

# Paragraph

The sun's own light is always the same white. What changes is the journey. A beam that comes straight down through the air keeps almost all of its colour, but a beam that has to slant in at the horizon crosses far thicker air, up to about 38 times as much. Air scatters blue more strongly than red, so on that long path the blue is thrown out of the beam and into the sky around you. What still reaches your eye is red and orange. That is the whole reason the setting sun looks red.

# Short

The colour of the direct sunlight depends on how much air the beam has crossed on its way to you. The sun's own light is always the same white. A beam that drops straight down through the sky crosses a column of air that weighs about one thousandth of the weight of all the air in the sky. A beam that comes in along the horizon must slant through up to about 38 times as much, because its path is so much longer.

Air scatters blue light more strongly than red light. On that long path the blue is scattered out of the beam and into the sky, and what is left to reach your eye is red and orange. Dust, smoke, salt and tiny water droplets scatter every colour about equally, and they add the pale haze that sits along the horizon. Where there is more of it, the sunset can look deeper red, because more blue is being thrown out of the beam.

The sun near the horizon looks flat rather than round, because the air bends the light and lifts the bottom of the disc more than the top. The bending is about half a degree, and the sun's disc is about half a degree across, so it looks squashed. The same bending keeps the last of the sun visible after it has truly set, which makes the day a little longer than the geometry alone says. After sunset the sky stays lit, because high air, far above the dust, is still scattering the sunlight that has dropped below the horizon. Last comes the blue hour, when the sun is far enough below the horizon that only the shorter, bluer wavelengths reach the ground.

# Medium

The light from the sun does not change colour. It is white, and it stays white. What changes is the journey the light makes on its way down to you.

That journey matters because the air is full of tiny particles, and those particles throw light about. Blue light is thrown about much more strongly than red light. Send a beam of sunlight straight down through the sky and it keeps most of its colour. Send the same beam along the horizon and most of the blue is knocked out of it before it reaches you.

The difference is in the amount of air. A beam aimed straight down crosses a column of air that weighs about one thousandth of the weight of all the air in the sky. A beam aimed along the horizon crosses up to about 38 times as much, because its path is so much longer.

![Two light paths through the atmosphere](/media/sunset-path-length.png)
*Two light paths: a short one running straight down through the air to the ground, and a long one slanting toward the horizon, where it has lost its blue.*

The long path is where the red comes from. As the slanting beam pushes through the thicker air, the blue in it is scattered sideways, into the sky and out of the beam. Red and orange survive. What reaches your eye at sunset is a beam with the blue taken away, so it looks red. The sun's light has not changed at all. The last thin slice of the disc is seen through the longest air path of all, so it goes deep red while the sky beside it is still yellow.

Dust, smoke, salt and tiny water droplets behave differently. They are far larger than the molecules that scatter blue, and they scatter every colour about equally. Their effect is the pale haze that sits along the horizon, and it is why a hazy evening looks washed out rather than fierce. Where there is more of it, more light is thrown sideways in every direction and the sunset can look deeper red.

The sun itself changes shape as it drops. Air bends light, and it bends the light coming up from the bottom of the sun's disc more than the light coming from the top. The bending is about half a degree, and the disc is about half a degree across, so the disc looks squashed into a shape that is wider than it is tall.

The same bending has a strange effect on the length of the day. Because the air lifts the sun's image, the last of the sun stays visible after the sun has truly set. A sunset ends a little later than the geometry of the sky alone would tell you.

Opposite the sun, in the east, the Earth's own shadow rises into the sky with a soft edge. Just above it lies a narrow pink band, called the Belt of Venus, which is sunlight that has travelled through a long path of air beyond the edge of the shadow and lost most of its blue.

And the sky does not go dark straight away. High in the air, far above the dust, the sunlight that has already dropped below the horizon is still being scattered. That light keeps the upper sky glowing for a while after the sun has gone. The blue hour comes near the end of it, when the sun is far enough below the horizon that only the shorter, bluer wavelengths reach the ground.

Meanwhile, the whole sky is doing what [a blue sky](/post/why-the-sky-is-blue) does at midday, only with less blue left in the beam to scatter.

# Long

Sunlight is white. That is not an impression: the light leaving the sun carries all the colours of the rainbow together. It is white before it leaves and white after it lands. Nothing about the sun turns red in the evening.

What decides the colour you see is the air the beam crossed on the way. That is the whole of the trick; the rest is only the detail.

## How much air a beam crosses

Air is full of molecules, and molecules are good at throwing light about. Blue light, with its short waves, is thrown about much more strongly than red light, with its long ones. Send a beam straight down through the sky and it arrives with nearly all of its colour intact. Send the same beam along the horizon and it arrives with much of the blue knocked out of it.

The difference between the two journeys is mostly distance, and distance here means air. A beam aimed straight down at the ground crosses a column of air that weighs about one thousandth of the weight of all the air in the sky. A beam travelling along the horizon crosses up to about 38 times as much, because its path is so much longer.

That extra air is not spread evenly along the beam. Almost all of it lies in the lowest few kilometres, where the air is thickest and the molecules are most crowded. The long, slanting beam therefore does its scattering almost at once, near the ground, rather than spread thinly along the way.

## The long path loses its blue

On the long path the blue is scattered out of the beam and into the sky. Red and orange are scattered less, so they travel on through the air and reach your eye. What you see at sunset is a beam with the blue taken away from it.

That is worth stating plainly, because the usual telling gets it backwards. The sky around the sun does not turn red because red light is painted on the sun. The sun's light is still white. The air in front of it works like a sieve, and the sieve keeps the red.

The light that was scattered sideways is not lost either. It is the light that lights the sky. Near the horizon the sky holds a red-orange glow, higher up it holds more pink and then blue, because the beam that reaches the upper sky has been emptied of even more of its blue.

The sun's own colour at the very edge of the horizon also comes from the same effect. The last thin slice of the disc is seen through the longest air path of all, and it goes deep red while the sky around it is still yellow.

## The layers of the air

![The layers of the atmosphere](/media/atmospheric-layers.png)
*The layers of the atmosphere from the ground up: the troposphere to about 12 km, the stratosphere to about 50 km with the ozone marked, the mesosphere to about 85 km, and the thermosphere above.*

The picture shows how the air is stacked. The troposphere runs from the ground to about 12 km. Everything we call weather happens here, and so does nearly all the dust, smoke, salt and water vapour. The stratosphere reaches about 50 km and holds the layer of ozone that absorbs much of the harmful sunlight. The mesosphere runs on to about 85 km, and above that lies the thermosphere, where the air is so thin that it is very nearly space.

The layers matter for sunsets because the air gets thinner fast with height. Nearly all of the mass of the atmosphere sits in the bottom layer, so the scattering that removes the blue from a horizontal beam happens mostly in the troposphere, low down.

They matter for a second reason as well. Once the sun has set for the ground, the air high above is still in sunlight. The high air, far above the dust, keeps scattering that light, and the sky stays lit after the sun has gone.

## Dust, smoke, salt and water

Some particles scatter blue strongly. Others do not. Molecules of air are small enough to throw short waves about far more readily than long ones. Dust, smoke, salt and tiny water droplets are much larger, and they scatter every colour about equally.

That equal scattering is what gives the horizon its pale haze. The haze is not a separate thing added to the sunset; it is the light of the sun, bounced in every direction by particles too big to favour one colour. Where there is more of it, the sky near the horizon goes milkier, and the red of the sun can deepen at the same time, because more of the beam's blue is being thrown out of it.

Water droplets are special, because a drop is a small lens. It bends and splits the light that falls on it, which is how [a rainbow](/post/how-a-rainbow-forms) forms. A droplet in a sunset path is doing something else as well, throwing light in every direction at once, and that is part of the haze.

## The squashed sun

The sun does not sit on the horizon as a clean circle. As it drops, the air bends the light coming up from the bottom of its disc more strongly than the light coming from the top, and lifts the lower edge. The sun looks flatter than it is, a little wider than it is tall.

The numbers are close. The bending at the horizon is about half a degree, and the disc of the sun is about half a degree across. The two measurements sit almost on top of each other, which is why the flattening is easy to see rather than something you have to measure.

The bending does more than squash the shape. It lifts the whole image of the sun, so the last of the sun is still visible after the sun has truly set. The day is a little longer than the geometry alone would tell you, and the evening a little longer than the clock on the wall seems to promise.

## After the sun has set

The sky does not go black the moment the sun goes. High air, far above the dust, is still lit by a sun that has dropped below the horizon for the ground below. That light is scattered in the high layers and falls back down onto the world as a soft glow with no disc in it.

The glow fades as the sun sinks further, because the high air is in shadow too. What remains longest is the light that has travelled furthest through the upper air, which is the reddest of all.

## The shadow in the east

Stand with your back to the setting sun and look east. There, opposite the sun, you can see the Earth's own shadow rising into the sky. It is the shadow the Earth casts on the air above, and its edge is soft and clear.

Just above that shadow lies a narrow pink band, called the Belt of Venus. It is sunlight that has passed through the long path of air beyond the edge of the shadow and had most of its blue scattered out of it, so what reaches your eye there is pink.

## The blue hour

Last of all comes the blue hour. It is the short time when the sun is far enough below the horizon that only the shorter, bluer wavelengths reach the ground. The reds are gone from the direct light, the sky is mostly blue, and everything in the street is lit by the sky rather than by the sun.

Then the light goes, and the day is over.

-> The sun did not change colour. The air changed the colour on the way down, and it will do it again tomorrow.
