# Why the Moon Changes Shape

# Sentence

The Moon is always half lit by the Sun; as it travels around the Earth we see more or less of that lit half, which is why it goes through the phases.

# Paragraph

The Moon is always half lit by the Sun. What changes from day to day is how much of that lit half we can see from the Earth. As the Moon travels round the Earth, the visible slice grows and shrinks, and the Moon passes through eight phases: new moon, crescent, first quarter, gibbous, full moon, gibbous again, last quarter and crescent again, before the pattern starts over after about 29.5 days. A full moon rises as the Sun sets, so it hangs in the sky all night. A new moon rises with the Sun, so it cannot be seen at all.

# Short

The Moon is always half lit by the Sun. Its shape seems to change because, as it travels around the Earth, we see a different part of that lit half each night. The Moon itself never changes the light it receives.

![The eight phases of the Moon in a row](/media/moon-phases.png#center)
*The eight phases in order, from the new moon through the crescent, the first quarter, the gibbous and the full moon and back again, each drawn as a disc with the lit part shaded.*

The eight phases follow the Moon's journey round the Earth: new moon, crescent, first quarter, gibbous, full moon, gibbous, last quarter and crescent again, and then a new moon. The whole cycle takes about 29.5 days, and it is called the synodic month.

A full moon rises as the Sun sets, so it hangs in the sky all night. A new moon rises with the Sun and sets with it, so it cannot be seen at all. The two half-moons, the first quarter and the last quarter, rise around noon and around midnight.

Quarter moons look like a disc split straight down the middle, and gibbous means more than half, with the bulge on one side. The change is slow enough to watch over a single evening. The full moon sits opposite the Sun in the sky, while the new moon sits beside it and is lost in the daylight glare.

The Moon's orbit is tilted by about 5 degrees to the plane of the Earth's orbit, so at new moon it usually passes above or below the Sun and no eclipse happens. An eclipse needs the three bodies almost in a line.

The Moon is about 400,000 kilometres away, roughly a quarter of the width of the Earth. It also turns once for every trip round the Earth, which is called tidal locking, so the same face is always turned towards us. The far side is not a dark side. It gets sunlight as much as the near side does.

The moonlight is sunlight reflected, and the full moon is about 400,000 times fainter than the Sun.

# Medium

The Moon looks different every week, but the Moon itself is not changing. It is always half lit by the Sun, the same half, and it is lit equally all the time. What changes from night to night is how much of that lit half happens to face the Earth.

Draw the Sun on the left, the Earth in the middle, and the Moon at four positions around it. At every one of those positions the half of the Moon that faces the Sun is lit, and the half turned away from it is dark.

![The Sun, the Earth and the Moon at four positions](/media/moon-shadow-geometry.png)
*The Sun on the left, the Earth in the middle, and the Moon at four positions around it. At each position the half of the Moon that faces the Sun is marked as lit and the shadowed half as dark.*

Looked at from the Earth, each of those positions shows a different amount of the lit half. When the Moon sits between the Earth and the Sun, its lit half faces away from us, and the Moon is new. When it sits on the far side, the lit half faces us directly, and the Moon is full. Halfway between, we see one half lit and one half dark, which is what makes a quarter moon.

Follow the Moon round the Earth and the phases fall into a fixed order: new moon, crescent, first quarter, gibbous, full moon, gibbous, last quarter, crescent, and new moon again after about 29.5 days. That cycle is called the synodic month. Gibbous means more than half, and it is the bulging shape you see on either side of the full moon.

A full moon rises as the Sun sets, so it stays in the sky all night. A new moon rises with the Sun and sets with the Sun, so it never climbs above the horizon on its own and cannot be seen at all. The first quarter rises around noon, and the last quarter around midnight.

Watch a single phase over a week of nights and you can follow it step by step. The crescent grows for about a week, the lit part swells towards the full moon, then the shape shifts across to the other side of the disc and shrinks away again until only a thin sliver is left. A few nights after that, the cycle begins again with a new moon.

If the Moon's orbit sat in the same plane as the Earth's orbit, every new moon would bring an eclipse. It does not. The Moon's orbit is tilted by about 5 degrees to the plane of the Earth's orbit, so at new moon it usually passes above or below the Sun and nothing much happens. An eclipse needs the three bodies almost in a line. The Earth's own tilt, which is about 23 degrees, is a separate thing, and that is the one that gives us [the seasons](/post/why-we-have-seasons).

The Moon turns once for each trip round the Earth, which is called tidal locking. That is why the same face is always turned towards us. The far side is not a dark side. It gets sunlight as much as the near side does, and it is only ever out of our sight.

The Moon is about 400,000 kilometres away, roughly a quarter of the width of the Earth. The light it sends us is sunlight reflected off grey rock and dust, and the full moon is about 400,000 times fainter than the Sun.

None of this changes the Moon itself. The distance stays about the same, the size in the sky stays about the same, and the lit half stays lit. What moves is our line of sight, and the phases are simply what that line of sight looks like.

# Long

There are nights when the Moon is a thin sliver and nights when it is a wide bright disc, and it is easy to think the Moon itself is changing shape. It is not. The Moon is always half lit by the Sun — one half of it, lit equally, all the time. The only thing that changes is how much of that lit half happens to be turned towards the Earth.

That single idea explains all eight phases, and it is worth holding on to before naming any of them, because the names are easy to mix up and the geometry behind them is very simple.

## The Sun, the Earth and four positions

Imagine a diagram. Put the Sun on the left, the Earth in the middle, and the Moon at four positions around the Earth: between the Earth and the Sun, above the Earth, below the Earth, and on the far side.

At every one of those four positions the half of the Moon that faces the Sun is lit, and the half turned away from the Sun is dark. The lit half always points the same way in space, towards the Sun.

Now look from the Earth. When the Moon is between the Earth and the Sun, its lit half faces away from us and we see only the dark half, so the Moon is new. When it is on the far side of the Earth, the lit half faces straight back at us and we see all of it, so the Moon is full. Above and below the Earth, we see half of the lit half and half of the dark half, and that is a quarter moon.

The Moon is not lit in any special way at any of these positions. It simply goes round the Earth, and the angle between it, us and the Sun changes as it goes. The shape you see is the answer to a question of geometry, not a change in the Moon.

## The eight names, and the synodic month

Following the Moon around from the new moon gives the familiar sequence: new moon, crescent, first quarter, gibbous, full moon, gibbous, last quarter, crescent, and new moon again.

A crescent is the sliver that grows between the new moon and the first quarter, and the shrinking sliver between the last quarter and the next new moon. Gibbous means more than half, and it is the bulging shape on either side of the full moon. The two quarter phases are the two halves in between.

The time from one new moon to the next is about 29.5 days, and it is called the synodic month. That is the number people mean when they say the Moon goes round in a month, and it is why the phases come back at roughly the same point in every calendar month. The Moon's turn against the starry background takes slightly less, about 27.3 days, because the Earth is moving along its own orbit at the same time.

## Rising and setting

A full moon rises as the Sun sets and sets as the Sun rises, so it hangs in the sky through the whole night. A new moon rises with the Sun and sets with the Sun, so it never climbs above the horizon on its own and cannot be seen at all. It is there, of course, in the same way the day is there, but it is lost in the glare of the Sun.

The first quarter rises around noon and is high in the afternoon, and the last quarter rises around midnight and is high towards morning. Between those two rise times sit the crescents and the gibbous shapes.

The Moon also rises a little later each night than the one before, roughly fifty minutes later, because it moves about thirteen degrees round the Earth every day. That is why the full moon of one month is a little later in the evening than the full moon of the month before.

## Why eclipses are rare

If the Moon's orbit lay in the same plane as the Earth's orbit, then every new moon would push in front of the Sun and every full moon would slide through the Earth's shadow. We would get an eclipse twice a month, every month.

It does not happen, because the Moon's orbit is tilted by about 5 degrees to the plane of the Earth's orbit. Most of the time, at new moon, the Moon passes above the Sun or below it rather than in front of it, and at full moon it passes above or below the Earth's shadow. An eclipse needs the three bodies almost in a line, which happens only when the Moon crosses that tilted plane at the right point. So eclipses come in clusters a few times a year rather than on a regular monthly beat.

The Earth's own tilt, of about 23 degrees away from the upright, is a different angle altogether, and it is the one that gives us our seasons rather than our eclipses.

## One face, always turned towards us

The Moon turns once for every trip it makes round the Earth, about 27.3 days, and it does so in step with that trip. This is called tidal locking. The pull of the Earth's tides has held the same face towards us for a very long time.

That is why the same face is always turned towards us, and it is also why the far side is so often called the dark side. It is a bad name. The far side gets sunlight as much as the near side does, and it is lit just as brightly. It is dark only in the sense that we cannot see it. When the near side is full, the far side has its own new moon, and when the near side is new, the far side is full.

## A faint light, a long way off

The Moon is about 400,000 kilometres away, on average, and it is roughly a quarter of the width of the Earth. Its light is not its own. It is sunlight reflected off grey rock and dust, which is why the full moon is about 400,000 times fainter than the Sun, even though the Moon looks big and bright in the sky.

A full moon can sometimes look blue or coppery rather than white. That is the Earth's own air getting in the way, with dust or water vapour scattering the light on its way down, and it is the same scattering that decides [the colour of the sky](/post/why-the-sky-is-blue) at sunrise and sunset.

The Moon is also a useful mirror for the Sun. The brightness of the full moon tells us how much sunlight reaches us unchanged, and it is one of the reasons astronomers watch the Moon carefully, long after the question of its phases was settled.

## Watching it yourself

Watching the phases is easier than it sounds. Pick a fixed spot with a clear view and look at the same time each evening. In the days after the new moon there is nothing to see at all, and then a thin crescent appears low down after sunset. It fattens night after night. Around the seventh day, half of the disc is lit and the Moon sits higher in the afternoon sky. A couple of nights later it is more than half, and by the time it is full it rises as the Sun goes down and stays up all night. After that it wanes in the same steady way, and a month later you are back where you started.

So the shape that seems to change from night to night is the shape of our own view. The Moon is always half lit, always the same size, and always the same distance. Only the angle moves, and the phases are what that angle looks like.
