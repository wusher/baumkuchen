# Why the Sky Is Blue

# Sentence

The sky is blue because air molecules scatter short wavelengths of sunlight far more strongly than long ones, so blue light reaches us from every direction of the sky at once.

# Paragraph

Sunlight looks white when it comes straight from the sun itself, but the sky is blue because the air gets in the way. Air molecules are far smaller than the wavelength of visible light, and they scatter it in every direction. This is called Rayleigh scattering, and its strength goes as 1/λ⁴: the shorter the wavelength, the far stronger the scatter. Blue light at about 450 nm is scattered about 4.4 times more strongly than red light at about 650 nm. We see blue because the scattered light arrives from the whole dome of sky, not from one place. Look at the sun itself and the beam is barely scattered, so the sun looks white.

# Short

Sunlight is white before it enters the air. It is a mixture of every visible wavelength, from about 400 to 700 nm, all travelling together in one beam. The sky is blue because the air between you and the sun throws the blue out of that beam. Air molecules are far smaller than the wavelength of visible light, and they do not swallow the light so much as push it sideways. A ray that passes close to a molecule leaves in a new direction, and the same happens all around it, so light spreads in every direction. This is called Rayleigh scattering. The strength of that scattering depends sharply on colour. It goes as 1/λ⁴, so the shorter the wavelength, the far stronger the scatter. Blue light at about 450 nm is scattered about 4.4 times more strongly than red light at about 650 nm.

Now put the observer in the picture. You do not look at one patch of scattered light. You look through the whole dome of sky at once, and every part of it sends you a little of the blue that was thrown out of the sunlight on the way in. Add up all those directions and the sky reads blue. Look back at the sun itself and the beam has barely been scattered, so the sun looks white.

The sky is not a pure blue, either. Close to the sun it goes white, because the light there is scattered mostly forwards and dust in the air scatters every colour alike. Near the horizon the sky is pale and hazy. Violet is present in the scattered light, but the eye is less sensitive to it and the sun's spectrum carries less of it, so the sky reads as blue rather than violet. Take the air away altogether and the blue goes with it: space is black, and on the Moon the sky stays black in the daytime for the same reason.

# Medium

Sunlight is white before it reaches the air. One beam carries every visible wavelength, from about 400 to 700 nm, mixed together in the same proportion. Nothing in that beam is blue on its own.

The air changes it. Air molecules are far smaller than the wavelength of visible light, and they push the light aside rather than take it in. A ray passing close to a molecule leaves in a new direction, and the same happens in every direction at once, so light spreads out in all directions. This is called Rayleigh scattering.

Scattering changes where the light goes, not what colour it is. The beam that still reaches the ground is barely altered, so it keeps the colour it started with. The air has also thrown out copies of that beam in every direction, and every copy has been through the same filter: less red, more blue. So the light arriving from a patch of sky arrives blue-tinted, while the light arriving straight from the sun has barely been touched.

Now the geometry. The sky is blue because the scattered light arrives from the whole dome of sky, not from one place. When you look up, every patch of air around you is sending a little blue light towards your eye at the same time. You are looking at the entire dome at once, and every part of it is blue, so the sum of it is blue.

Turn round and look at the sun itself and you get white. The beam coming straight at you has barely been scattered on the way; the scattered light went sideways, out of your line of sight.

The sky is not a pure blue, either. Close to the sun it goes white, because light scattered mostly forwards keeps much of its own colour, and dust in the air scatters every colour alike. At the horizon the sky is pale and hazy, because the light has crossed a great deal of air and been scattered many times, mixing the colours back together. Violet is present in the scattered light, but the eye is less sensitive to it and the sun's spectrum carries less of it, so the sky reads as blue.

Take the air away and the blue goes with it. Space is black, because with no air there is nothing to scatter the light. An astronaut sees a black sky and a very bright sun. On the Moon the sky stays black in the daytime for the same reason: no air, no scattering, so the sky above the bright ground stays dark.

The sun's own light is about 5800 K, which is why the sun itself looks slightly yellow from space. From the ground it looks white, because the air in between adds blue to the direct beam. And when the sun sits low, the long path through the air scatters much of the blue out of the beam, leaving the red and orange behind — see [a red sunset](/post/why-sunsets-are-red).

# Long

Start with the light itself. Sunlight is white, and it is white before it has touched the air around us: a single beam carrying every visible wavelength, from about 400 to 700 nm, mixed together in the same proportion. Nothing in that beam is blue on its own account. White is simply what you get when all of those arrive together.

Then the air gets involved. Air is not empty. It is full of molecules, and each molecule is far smaller than the wavelength of visible light. A sunbeam passing through it does not travel straight. It grazes molecules, and each grazing throws a little of the light in a new direction. The beam is still there and still carries the whole mixture, but a copy of it now spreads outwards in all directions. This spreading is called scattering, and because the molecules are so much smaller than the light, it is called Rayleigh scattering.

## How steeply the scattering depends on colour

How much light goes into each of those directions depends sharply on the wavelength, and the rule is startlingly steep. The strength of the scattering goes as 1/λ⁴: the shorter the wavelength, the far stronger the scatter, and the effect steepens fast as the wavelength drops. That is why the sky is not merely a bit blue. It is blue because blue light is scattered so much more strongly than red light that it dominates what reaches your eye.

Work out the difference for the colours that matter. Blue light at about 450 nm is scattered about 4.4 times more strongly than red light at about 650 nm. Everything in between sits in between: violet more strongly than blue, red least of all.

The rule cuts both ways, and that matters for everything else here. Whatever is short in wavelength is scattered strongly, and whatever is long is scattered weakly, so the sky keeps a bias towards the short end of the visible range no matter how thin the air is. Red light survives a long journey through the atmosphere almost untouched. Blue light does not, and it is pulled out of the direct beam long before that beam reaches you.

## Why the whole sky matters

Now the part that is easy to get wrong. You might expect to see a blue patch of sky in the direction of the sun and grey everywhere else. That is not what happens, and the reason is the geometry of what you are looking at.

The sky is blue because the scattered light arrives from the whole dome of sky, not from one place. When you look up, every patch of air above and around you is throwing blue light towards your eye at the same time. The blue is not located in one spot; it is a direction, and you are looking along every one of those directions at once. The dome is blue all over, so the sum of everything you receive is blue.

Look straight at the sun instead and the answer changes. That beam has come almost straight across, and it has not been scattered much on the way. The light that was scattered went sideways, away from your eye. So the sun looks white.

There is a second reason you never see a single bright blue spot in the direction of the sun. The blue light reaching you from overhead has usually been scattered more than once, on its way down through the air as well as on its way up. Every scattering sends it in a new direction. One scattering would leave a blue smear around the sun's own position. Many scatterings spread that light over the whole dome instead, which is why the sky is the same blue all the way round rather than brightest where the sun is.

## Where the blue is not

There are two places where the sky stops being a clean blue. Look near the sun on a clear day and the sky there is white, not blue. Light scattered almost straight forwards is not so strongly tinted by wavelength, and dust in the air, which is much bigger than a single molecule, scatters every colour alike. That dust adds a white haze to the sky around the sun.

Look towards the horizon and the blue turns pale instead. The light there has travelled through a great deal of air and has been scattered many times on the way, and repeated scattering mixes the colours back together rather than leaving a pure blue behind. You are also looking through more air than you do when you look up, so a good deal of that light has been scattered out of the line of sight before it reaches you.

And violet? It is there in the scattered light. But the eye is less sensitive to violet than to blue, and the sun's spectrum carries less of it to begin with, so the sky reads as blue rather than violet.

![The sky around a low sun](/media/sky-color-angle.png)
*The sky around a low sun, bright and white close to it and deepening to blue further away, with the colour changing with the angle you look at.*

Notice what that picture shows about angles. The white close to the sun is the direct beam, hardly scattered at all, and it sits on top of the blue that comes from everywhere else. Mixed together, they wash out near the sun and leave the blue on its own further away.

## No air, no sky

Take the air away and the blue goes with it. Space is black, because with no air there is nothing there to scatter the light. An astronaut in orbit sees a black sky and a very bright sun, with no glow around it at all.

On the Moon the sky stays black in the daytime, for exactly the same reason. There is no air to do the scattering, so the sunlight reaches the ground almost unchanged and the sky above the horizon stays dark while the ground below is brightly lit.

The contrast with Earth is worth sitting with. From the Moon, the Earth hangs in that black sky as a bright blue marble. It is blue for the same reason the sky is blue here, seen from far enough away that the whole atmosphere is folded into a single dot.

## The colour of the sun

Even the sun is not quite what it seems. The light of the sun's surface is about 5800 K, which makes it slightly yellow rather than perfectly white. Seen from space, where no air lies in the way, that is the colour you see. Seen from the ground, the air in between scatters some of the blue out of the direct beam and adds a little blue haze of its own, and the two together make the sun look white. The air is doing part of the work of colouring the sun for us.

## The same rule everywhere else

The same rule reaches further than the sky. When the sun is low, its light has a long way to travel through the air before it reaches you, and by then much of the blue has been scattered out of the beam. What is left in the direct beam is red and orange, which is the whole subject of a red sunset.

Water droplets bend light too, and they bend the short wavelengths more strongly than the long ones, spreading the same set of colours out into an arc. That arc is [the rainbow](/post/how-a-rainbow-forms), and it is the same ordering of wavelengths you can see in the sky, laid out along a curve instead of across a dome.
