# Why Thunder Follows Lightning

# Sentence

Lightning heats its channel to about 30,000 degrees in an instant, and the air around that channel explodes outward as a shock wave, so the flash arrives first and the thunder follows behind it.

# Paragraph

The channel is heated to about 30,000 degrees Celsius in a few millionths of a second. # Short

Lightning is a discharge of electricity. Inside a storm cloud, a faint channel called a stepped leader begins to creep downward. It does not glow much. It zigzags, and it branches. When the tip of that channel reaches the ground, a bright return stroke runs back up the same path, and that is the flash you see.

The flash comes with heat. The channel is heated to about 30,000 degrees Celsius in a few millionths of a second, and the air inside it is briefly a plasma, with its electrons stripped away from the atoms. Air cannot heat up that fast without expanding violently. That sudden expansion is the shock wave, and the shock wave is thunder.

So why does the thunder always come after the flash? Because light crosses about 300,000 kilometres a second, and sound in air at 20 degrees Celsius crosses about 343 metres a second. Light is close to a million times quicker. The flash arrives at once. The bang arrives late, and the gap between the two tells you how far away the storm is.

Count the seconds between flash and bang, then divide by three, and you have the distance in kilometres. Three seconds of delay means a storm about one kilometre away. Nine seconds means about three. The count is easy to do in the dark, because the flash always reaches you first.

One stroke is not a single straight line. It is a branching channel tens of kilometres long, and the sound from each part of it reaches the ground by a slightly different route. Close by, those sounds arrive almost together and the thunder cracks. Far away, they arrive spread out, and the thunder rumbles.

# Medium

Lightning is a discharge of electricity, and it is one of the strangest things the air does. A storm cloud slowly sorts its charge: ice crystals and water droplets collide inside the cloud, and positive charge collects near the top while negative charge gathers near the bottom.

A faint channel then starts to creep down from the cloud. It is called a stepped leader, because it comes down in steps, and it branches as it goes. The leader is dim. You will not notice it in the sky. It works its way towards the ground, and it can use the rain beneath the cloud to make its final connection.

When the leader finally touches down, a bright return stroke runs back up the same channel. That stroke is the flash. It carries a current of tens of thousands of amperes, and it is the brightest thing in the storm by a wide margin.

The flash and the bang come from the same event, but they travel in very different ways. Light crosses about 300,000 kilometres a second. Sound in air at 20 degrees Celsius crosses about 343 metres a second, close to a million times slower. The light arrives at once. The sound arrives later, and the gap between them grows with the distance.

Why is there a sound at all? Because the channel is heated to about 30,000 degrees Celsius in a few millionths of a second. The air inside that channel is briefly a plasma, with its electrons stripped away from the atoms. Air cannot heat up that fast without expanding violently. That sudden expansion shoves the surrounding air outward, and the shove spreads through the atmosphere as a shock wave. We hear the shock wave as thunder.

So the answer to the title question is plain: the thunder is made at the same moment as the flash, but it has a great deal further to travel. A current running along the channel also makes a magnetic field around it, in the same way that a current in a wire does, and that bridge between electricity and magnetism is worth a look in [how magnets work](/post/how-magnets-work).

Near the ground, the same storm gives two different sounds. Close by, the report is a crack, sharp and sudden. Far away, it is a rumble that rolls on for several seconds. A stroke is not one straight line. It is a branching channel tens of kilometres long, and the sound from each part of it reaches you by a slightly different route. The parts arrive almost together when the storm is close, and spread out when it is far away.

The leader is worth one more look, because it is the part you never see. It carries most of the journey on its own, branching down through the cloud and reaching for the ground through the falling rain. The bright stroke you watch is only the last part of the journey, run backwards in a single blaze.

Next time a storm comes over, count the seconds between the flash and the bang. Divide by three and you have the distance in kilometres.

# Long

Lightning arrives all at once, and then, a moment later, it speaks. That gap between the two is the whole subject of this piece, and it is a small mystery with a very plain answer at the end of it.

## What a flash really is

A lightning flash is a discharge of electricity. Not a small one, either. The channel it runs along carries a current of tens of thousands of amperes for a moment that is far shorter than you could measure by eye.

Before any of that happens, the storm cloud has been sorting itself out. Inside the cloud, ice crystals and water droplets collide again and again. The collisions strip electrons off the droplets and leave them behind. Positive charge gathers towards the top of the cloud, negative charge towards the bottom, and the cloud becomes a set of enormous electric fields.

Then a discharge begins. A faint channel starts to creep down from the cloud base. This is the stepped leader, and the name fits it well, because it does not travel in a straight run. It comes down in steps, and it branches as it goes, leaving a thin, dim thread of conducting air behind it. On its own the leader is far too dull to notice from the ground. It works downwards through the cloud and then through the rain beneath it, and it uses the water as the easiest path to the ground.

When the tip of the leader finally reaches the ground, the ground and the cloud are joined by a conducting path for the first time. A bright return stroke then runs back up that path, from the ground to the cloud, and it is the brightest thing in the storm by a wide margin.

## The hottest thing you will not see

The channel is heated to about 30,000 degrees Celsius in a few millionths of a second. That is roughly five times the temperature of the surface of the Sun, reached in less time than it takes to blink.

The air in that channel is briefly a plasma. At a temperature like that, the electrons are stripped away from their atoms and left free to move. The gas is no longer gas in the ordinary sense; it is a soup of charged particles, and it conducts electricity well enough to carry a current of tens of thousands of amperes.

You never look at that heat directly. What you see as the flash is the light from the hot gas along the channel, and it is bright enough to hurt, to bleach the colour out of a photograph, and to leave an afterimage that fades over the next few seconds.

## Why the air has to explode

Here is the part that makes the sound. Air cannot heat up that fast without expanding violently. In a kitchen, a pan of air warms slowly and the molecules simply shuffle about faster. In a lightning channel there is no time for that. The air is slammed from a mild day to thirty thousand degrees Celsius in a blink, and it has nowhere to go.

So it goes outward. The hot gas shoves the cooler air around it, and that push travels outward through the atmosphere as a shock wave. The shock wave is thunder.

Thunder is not a separate event that happens after the lightning. The push starts at the same instant the channel lights up. By the time you see the flash, the shock wave is already on its way, but it is slow, and you have not felt it yet.

## Light is quick and sound is not

Light crosses about 300,000 kilometres a second. Sound in air at 20 degrees Celsius crosses about 343 metres a second, which is a bit over 1,200 kilometres an hour. One of those numbers is close to a million times bigger than the other.

That single fact decides everything you observe about a storm. The flash crosses the country and the sea and arrives at once, whether the storm is two kilometres away or twenty. The bang has to cross the same distance through the same air, and by the time it reaches you the lightning is long over.

## Measuring the storm

The delay between the flash and the bang is a ruler laid across the sky. If you know the speed of sound, you know the distance.

The rule of thumb takes the arithmetic away: count the seconds between flash and bang, then divide by three, and you have the distance in kilometres. Three seconds of delay means a storm about one kilometre away. Six seconds means two kilometres. Nine seconds means three.

![A scale that ties a delay in seconds to a distance in kilometres](/media/flash-to-bang.png)
*A scale for the gap: count the seconds between flash and bang, and divide by three to get the distance in kilometres.*

The rule of thumb is close rather than exact. Sound moves a little faster in warm air than in cold, and it bends as it travels, so some of the bang arrives by a longer route than the straight line to the cloud. Use it as a good estimate, not a measurement.

You do not need to count in the dark. Stand outside and watch the horizon, where the flash usually shows up first. Then start counting, and keep counting through the gaps until the thunder arrives. If the storm is still counting, keep counting for the next flash.

## Rumbles and cracks

One stroke is not one straight line. It is a branching channel tens of kilometres long, and the sound from each part of it reaches the ground by a slightly different route. Some parts are closer than others. Some of the sound passes through warm air high up and bends its way down. Some of it bounces off hills, buildings and the ground itself before it arrives.

When the storm is close, all of that arrives within a fraction of a second, and the sounds pile up on top of each other. The result is a crack: sharp, sudden, and loud enough to rattle the windows. When the storm is far away, the same sounds arrive spread across several seconds, and you hear a rumble that rolls along instead of striking.

There is a second reason far thunder sounds different. The visible flash is the brightest part of the channel, and on a long stroke you may be seeing one branch while hearing another. The light that reaches you and the sound that reaches you do not have to come from the same piece of the storm.

## The rest of the sky

A thunderstorm also changes the light around it, and the change is easier to understand than the noise. The cloud and the rain scatter sunlight in every direction, which turns the underside of a storm grey and flat. Between showers the sky keeps its ordinary blue, for the reasons set out in [the colour of the sky](/post/why-the-sky-is-blue).

The rain itself is the same water that falls from any cloud. Nothing about it is special until it is falling through the lightning channel, and when it is, the air around it has been heated far beyond anything the surface of the Earth ever reaches.

## Listening for the gap

So thunder follows lightning because the two travel at completely different speeds. The flash is made and heard at once, because light is quick enough to cross any distance you care about. The bang is made at that same moment and arrives late, because sound moves close to a million times more slowly through the air.

Every storm is a clock, and the gap between the flash and the bang is the reading. Count the seconds, divide by three, and you know how far away the weather is.
