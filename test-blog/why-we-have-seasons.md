# Why We Have Seasons

# Sentence

The Earth spins on an axis tilted 23.4 degrees, so each hemisphere leans towards the Sun in turn. That changes the Sun's height and the length of the day, and both bring more heat.

# Paragraph

The Earth's axis leans 23.4 degrees away from the perpendicular to its orbit, and it keeps leaning that way as the Earth goes round the Sun. The Moon's gravity helps to hold the tilt steady. Because the axis stays tilted, the northern hemisphere faces the Sun more directly for half the year and less directly for the other half. When it faces the Sun more directly, the Sun climbs higher in the sky and the days run longer. Both of those bring more heat, and that is what we call summer. The southern hemisphere gets the opposite, so its summer comes when ours is winter.

# Short

Perpendicular means at right angles: a line at right angles to the orbit would point straight at the Sun, and ours leans 23.4 degrees away from that. The Moon's gravity helps to hold the tilt steady. Because the tilt never changes, the northern hemisphere turns to face the Sun more directly for half the year and less directly for the other half.

That is winter in the north and summer in the south, because the south pole leans the other way at the same time. The tilt is what makes the difference between them.

# Medium

The seasons have one cause, and it is a small one. The Earth's axis leans.

## A tilted axis

The axis is the imaginary line through the Earth's poles about which the planet turns once a day. It leans 23.4 degrees away from the perpendicular to the Earth's orbit round the Sun. Perpendicular means at right angles to the plane of that orbit, and a line at right angles would point straight at the Sun. Ours leans away from that by 23.4 degrees, and it leans the same way all year long. The Moon's gravity helps to keep it that way, so the tilt does not wander from one year to the next.

## The two positions in the year

Because the axis keeps its direction while the Earth travels round the Sun, the northern hemisphere faces the Sun more directly for half the year and less directly for the other half.

At one point in the orbit the north pole leans towards the Sun. The Sun climbs higher at midday and the days run longer. Both changes bring more heat, and that is northern summer.

At the opposite point the north pole leans away. The Sun sits lower and the days shorten. Less heat reaches the ground, and that is northern winter. The southern hemisphere leans the other way at both points, so its summer comes when ours is winter.

## Why a high Sun makes more heat

Here is the part worth drawing. Take one beam of sunlight and one square of ground. When the Sun is high, the beam falls steeply and lands on a small patch. When the Sun is low, the same beam spreads over a wide patch.

![Two ground diagrams side by side, one with a steep beam on a small patch and one with a shallow beam over a wide patch](/media/sun-angle.png#center)
*One beam of sunlight falls steeply on a small patch of ground when the Sun is high, and spreads over a wide patch when the Sun is low.*

The energy in the beam is the same either way. Spread over a wide patch, each square metre of ground gets less of it. So the noon heat in summer is much stronger than at the same hour in winter. The Sun is not only higher for longer; it is also pouring its energy onto less ground.

## Distance is not the cause

It is tempting to think the seasons come from the Earth moving nearer to and further from the Sun. It does move. The closest approach comes around 4 January, at about 147.1 million kilometres, and the farthest around 4 July, at about 152.1 million. But the northern summer comes at the farthest point of the year. If distance decided the seasons, the coldest weeks in the north would fall in January, and they do not. The tilt does the whole job.

## The living world feels it

Extra heat and longer days set off everything that grows. Grass greens up earlier, buds open weeks ahead, and trees leaf out while the nights are still cold. That is the machinery behind [how plants make food](/post/how-plants-make-food), and it runs on the extra hours of daylight as much as on the warmth.

## The equator and the poles

The tilt gives two very different kinds of season at the two ends of the Earth.

Near the equator the length of the day barely changes through the year, so the heat changes little too. What changes is the rain. The belt of wet weather moves north and south with the Sun's overhead point, and the wettest months follow it round the year. That is the wet season and the dry season.

Inside the polar circles the opposite happens. The Sun does not set at all at midsummer, and it does not rise at all at midwinter. There the tilt produces days that last for months and nights that do too.

# Long

## The axis is tilted

The Earth turns once a day on an axis that runs from the north pole to the south pole through the middle of the planet. That axis is tilted. It leans 23.4 degrees away from the perpendicular to the plane of the Earth's orbit round the Sun.

Perpendicular means at right angles. A line at right angles to the plane of the orbit would point straight at the Sun. Ours leans 23.4 degrees away from that, and it leans in the same direction for the whole year, month after month, as the Earth travels round its path.

The tilt is also steady over long times, and the Moon's gravity is a big part of why. Without the Moon pulling on the Earth, the axis would swing about more from year to year, and the seasons would be less orderly. The Moon is worth knowing on its own account too, and [the phases of the moon](/post/why-the-moon-changes-shape) is a good place to start with it.

## The two positions that matter

Because the axis holds its direction while the Earth travels round the Sun, the northern hemisphere turns to face the Sun more directly for half the year and less directly for the other half.

At one point in the orbit the north pole leans towards the Sun. The Sun climbs higher in the sky at midday and the days run longer. Both changes bring more heat, and that is the northern summer.

At the opposite point the north pole leans away from the Sun. The Sun sits lower in the sky at midday and the days shorten. Less heat reaches the ground, and that is the northern winter.

Nothing else about the Earth changes to mark these dates. The tilt is the same in January as it is in July. What changes is the direction from which the Sun arrives, and how long it stays above the horizon.

## A high Sun and a low Sun

Take a single beam of sunlight and a square of ground. When the Sun is high, the beam comes down steeply and lands on a small patch. When the Sun is low, the same beam is stretched out and covers a wide patch.

The beam carries the same energy either way. What changes is how much of it lands on each square metre. Spread over a wide patch, each square metre receives less. That is why the noon heat on a summer afternoon is much stronger than on a winter afternoon at the same hour, and it is why a low winter Sun can leave a room cold even on a clear day.

The two changes in the last section work together. A longer day gives the Sun more hours to warm the ground. A higher Sun puts more of that heat into each square metre of it. Neither alone would make the season; together they do.

## Distance from the Sun is not the cause

It is easy to guess that the seasons come from the Earth moving nearer to and further from the Sun. The Earth does move. Its closest approach falls around 4 January, at about 147.1 million kilometres, and its farthest around 4 July, at about 152.1 million.

Those two dates are close to the opposite of what the seasons require. The northern summer arrives when the Earth is at the farthest point of the year, and the northern winter arrives when it is at the closest. If distance drove the heat, the coldest weeks in the north would fall in January and the warmest would fall in July. It is the other way round.

The gap between those two distances is small beside the change the tilt makes, and the tilt also changes the length of the day. Distance alters the strength of the sunlight a little. The tilt alters both the strength and the hours, so the tilt wins.

## The length of the day

The second change is the length of the day, and it varies most across the Earth.

![A graph of the length of the day against latitude for the summer solstice and the winter solstice](/media/daylight-hours.png)
*The length of the day against latitude for the summer solstice and the winter solstice. Both curves cross about twelve hours at the equator, then part as they run towards the poles.*

At the equator the length of the day barely changes through the year. Both curves pass through about twelve hours there, and they stay close together for some distance either side of it. Further north and south the curves pull apart. In June the summer curve rises steeply towards the pole while the winter curve falls away. In December they swap over.

The gap between the curves is the whole of the seasonal change in daylight, and it grows as you move away from the equator. At the polar circles the summer curve reaches twenty-four hours while the winter curve reaches none, and at the poles themselves the two curves sit at the very top and the very bottom of the scale.

## The equator has seasons of another kind

Because the length of the day near the equator barely changes, the heat there changes far less through the year than it does in the north. What changes instead is the rain.

The Sun's heat drives winds that carry moisture from the tropics towards the poles, and those winds shift north and south over the year. As they shift, the belt of rain moves with them. A place near the equator may get its wettest weeks in one part of the year and its driest in another. That is the wet season and the dry season, and they are the seasons of the equatorial belt.

So the word season means different things in different places. In the north it mostly means cold and heat. Near the equator it means wet and dry.

## Inside the polar circles

The polar circles are the lines of latitude that sit 66.5 degrees north and south of the equator, right where the tilt just lets the Sun graze the horizon at its highest and its lowest.

Inside the northern polar circle the Sun does not set at all at midsummer. It circles the sky at midnight, low but always there. At midwinter it does not rise at all, and the daylight lasts no more than a short twilight around noon.

The same happens the other way round in the south, but with the months reversed, because the southern hemisphere leans towards the Sun when the northern one leans away.

## The four dates

The turning points come round once a year, and they are the dates most people already know:

- Around 20 March and again around 22 September, the Sun sits straight over the equator. Day and night are the same length everywhere on the Earth, and the length of the day is changing fastest. These are the equinoxes.
- Around 21 June the Sun is furthest north of the equator, and the northern hemisphere has its longest day. This is the June solstice.
- Around 21 December the Sun is furthest south of the equator, and the northern hemisphere has its shortest day. This is the December solstice.

The dates drift a little from year to year, because the Earth's orbit and its speed both vary slightly, but the order never changes. The longest day of the year always comes at the June solstice and the shortest at the December one.

## The southern hemisphere has the opposite seasons

When the north pole leans towards the Sun, the south pole leans away. The southern summer and the northern summer never arrive together.

In December, when it is winter in the north, it is summer in the south, and the days there run long. In June, when it is midsummer in the north, the south is in the middle of its winter.

The height of the Sun and the length of the day follow the same rule in both hemispheres. The only difference is which way the tilt leans at that point in the year, and that single difference is the whole of the southern opposite.
